Is Your Line Delivering What You Pay For? Speed Tests Done Right

How to measure your connection so the numbers mean something, what your contract really promises, and what to do in Austria when it falls short.

The contract says 150 Mbit/s. The speed test on the phone says 38. You run it again and get 112, and an hour later 61. When you finally call the hotline, the friendly person on the other end asks whether you were on Wi‑Fi, and the conversation goes nowhere, because you honestly do not know which of the three numbers was the real one.

The good news: there is a way to measure that produces numbers your provider has to take seriously, and in Austria the regulator even hands you the tool for free.

First, Read the Numbers Right

Speed tests and contracts both talk about megabits per second (Mbit/s). Download windows and file managers usually show megabytes per second (MB/s). One byte is eight bits, so divide by eight: a 100 Mbit/s line downloads at about 12 MB/s at best. Also, no test ever shows the full contract figure: every packet carries addressing and control data, so a perfect 100 Mbit/s line usually tests a few per cent lower. That is normal.

What Your Contract Actually Promises

Since 2016, an EU regulation requires providers to state several speeds in fixed‑line contracts, not just the headline number. You will find them in the contract summary or the service description:

  • Maximum speed: the best case. You should be able to reach it at least some of the time.
  • Normally available speed: what you should get most of the day. Austrian consumer guidance describes it as the speed available 95 per cent of the time, and it is the figure that matters most when you have a dispute.
  • Minimum speed: the floor. The line should never drop below it, apart from real faults.
  • Advertised speed: the number on the poster, often the same as the maximum.

For mobile internet, including 5G home routers, the rules are looser: the contract only needs an estimated maximum speed, because radio conditions change by the minute. Our guide to fibre, cable, DSL and 5G explains why the line type makes such a difference.

Quick win: Find your contract summary now and write the four numbers on a sticky note next to the router. Every later test is only meaningful against them.

How to Test So the Result Counts

A test from the sofa measures your Wi‑Fi, your phone and everything else that is downloading, and only then your line. To measure the line, remove the rest.

  1. Use a cable. Connect a computer to the router with a network cable. Wi‑Fi results say more about the room than about the connection, as why your Wi‑Fi is slow explains in detail.
  2. Stop the other traffic. Pause cloud backups, game downloads and streaming on other devices. Ideally, nobody else uses the connection during the test.
  3. Check the cable and the port. An old cable or a 100 Mbit/s port on an older laptop caps the result at around 94 Mbit/s, whatever the line can do.
  4. Test at different times. Morning, late afternoon and around 20:00 to 22:00, when the whole neighbourhood streams. A line that is fine at 10:00 and poor every evening points to congestion at the provider.
  5. Write everything down. Date, time, result and the test used. Screenshots alone get lost.

If the download looks fine but calls still stutter when someone uploads, the problem is probably latency rather than speed. That is a different measurement, described in our article on video calls and bufferbloat.

Which Test to Use

Tests differ in what they measure and how much weight their results carry.

Test Good for Limits Verdict
Quick browser test (Ookla Speedtest, fast.com) A first impression in 30 seconds One moment, one server, often over Wi‑Fi Fine for a hunch, useless as proof
RTR Netztest, single test (web or app) Neutral test run by the Austrian regulator, also for mobile Still a single snapshot The best everyday test in Austria
Loaded latency test (for example Cloudflare's speed test) Finding stutter and lag under load Says little about contract speeds Use it when calls suffer, not for disputes
RTR certified measurement Evidence for a complaint about a fixed line Takes three sessions on three days The one that counts when you want something done

The RTR Netztest map also shows what others measured nearby, which helps when the mobile signal at home is weak.

The Certified Measurement, Step by Step

The certified measurement (zertifizierte Messung) on netztest.at is the Austrian regulator RTR's tool for producing evidence. For fixed lines (DSL, cable and fibre), its results count as prima facie evidence in warranty questions. In plain words: if the measurement shows a shortfall, it is taken as a fact unless the provider can show convincingly why the line is fine after all. You can also use it for mobile home internet such as a 5G router with a flat rate, but there the result carries less weight.

According to RTR's current instructions, it works like this:

  1. Use a computer with an up‑to‑date browser. Phones and tablets are not supported for the certified measurement.
  2. Connect by cable if you can. If you must use Wi‑Fi, stay close to the access point.
  3. Fill in the form at the first session: name, address and details of your setup, plus photos of the computer and router. Do not change the setup between sessions.
  4. Run at least three measurement cycles on three different days within two weeks. Each cycle takes about an hour and 45 minutes and runs a test every 15 minutes on its own. Keep the computer awake, do not use it for anything else, and make sure nobody else at home is online.
  5. Check the count. You need at least 18 successful single measurements in total, and at least two in every cycle. If some fail, run another cycle.
  6. Download each PDF immediately. RTR does not keep them; close the window too early and that cycle is lost.
  7. Sign the PDFs. The courts and RTR's dispute resolution body may ask for printed, signed copies.

Worth knowing: The certified measurement uses a lot of data. Use it only on a line without a data cap, and do not expect it to document a complete outage: when the line is down, there is nothing to measure.

When the Numbers Fall Short

With the measurement in hand, the order of steps is simple:

  1. Contact the provider first, in writing, with the PDFs and the contract speeds you compare against. Often a technician visit or a new router solves it.
  2. Go to RTR's dispute resolution body (Schlichtungsstelle) if the provider refuses or nothing changes. It is free for customers and handles complaints about telecom providers.
  3. Know your options. Depending on the contract, a permanent shortfall can justify a price reduction, a switch to a cheaper tariff that matches the real speed, or ending the contract. Every case is judged on its own, so the measurement is evidence, not a guaranteed outcome.

For Small Businesses

An office depends on the line more than most homes, so measure before there is a dispute. A small monitoring setup that logs latency and packet loss around the clock shows patterns a single test never will. Business tariffs often include a service level agreement with repair times or guaranteed bandwidth; check what exactly it guarantees and how a breach is reported, because that is the document you will argue with.

Conclusion

A speed test is only as good as the way it is run. Read your contract's normally available and minimum speeds, test with a cable at different times of day, and keep a simple log. If the line keeps falling short, the RTR certified measurement turns your hunch into evidence: three sessions on three days, PDFs saved and signed, then a written complaint to the provider and, if needed, RTR's dispute resolution body. The next time the hotline asks whether you were on Wi‑Fi, you will already have the answer.
