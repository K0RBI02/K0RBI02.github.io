IPv6 at Home and in the Office: What Changes and What Doesn't

Your network probably already speaks IPv6. Here is what that means for addresses, security and everyday use.

IPv6 has been "the future" for so long that many people overlook a simple fact: for a large share of internet users, it is already the present. Many providers enable it by default, most operating systems prefer it, and a significant part of traffic to large services already runs over it. Chances are your network uses IPv6 right now.

Why IPv6 Exists

IPv4 has about 4.3 billion addresses — far too few for every phone, laptop, TV and sensor on the planet. For years the internet worked around the shortage with NAT (network address translation): your whole home shares one public IPv4 address, and the router keeps track of which internal device each connection belongs to.

Providers now go a step further and share one IPv4 address among many customers (CGNAT, DS‑Lite). That works for browsing but breaks anything that needs incoming connections. How to tell whether your own line is affected is covered in can't reach home from outside?

IPv6 solves the shortage at the root. Its address space is so large that every device can have its own globally unique address.

How Addressing Works

Instead of a single address, your provider assigns your router a whole prefix — typically a /56 or /48 for a business, sometimes a /64 for a household. The router then hands out addresses to your devices from it.

  • A /64 is one network segment with more addresses than you will ever use.
  • A /56 contains 256 such segments — enough to give every VLAN its own.

Devices usually configure their addresses themselves (SLAAC) based on the prefix the router announces. That is why IPv6 addresses look long and random: 2001:db8:4a2:1f00:8c1e:3b2f:9d41:7e05.

Worth knowing: Most devices also use temporary addresses for outgoing connections that change regularly. This prevents websites from tracking a device by a fixed address over time.

Security: NAT Was Never the Firewall

A common worry is that without NAT, every device is suddenly exposed. In practice, what protects you is the stateful firewall in your router, not NAT. Standard home routers block unsolicited incoming IPv6 connections by default and only allow replies to connections your devices started.

What changes is how you open access deliberately:

  • With IPv4 you set up port forwarding to an internal address.
  • With IPv6 you create a firewall rule that allows a specific port to a specific device. The router checklist shows where to look.

The same advice applies as with IPv4: open as little as possible, and prefer a VPN to exposing services directly.

What Can Go Wrong

  • Changing prefixes: Some providers assign a new prefix on every reconnect. Devices adapt automatically, but firewall rules and DNS entries that use full addresses break. Ask for a static prefix for business connections.
  • Only half configured: If IPv6 works on the router but a firewall or VPN only filters IPv4, traffic may bypass your rules. Treat both protocols equally in every policy.
  • Monitoring blind spots: Logging and inventory tools that only look at IPv4 miss part of what happens on the network.

Practical Advice

At home: leave IPv6 enabled, keep the router firewall on its defaults and use IPv6 to reach your own services from outside if you are stuck behind CGNAT — with a firewall rule, ideally to a VPN.

In the office: run dual-stack (IPv4 and IPv6 side by side), request a static prefix, give each VLAN its own /64, and make sure firewall rules, VPN and monitoring cover both protocols.

Conclusion

IPv6 does not make networks more dangerous or more complicated in daily use — it makes them simpler and restores the original idea that every device can be reached directly, when you allow it. The firewall still decides what gets in. Learn the few new concepts, treat both protocols the same, and IPv6 quietly does its job.
