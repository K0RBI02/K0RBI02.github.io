DNS: The Quiet Service Every Connection Depends On

When the internet "is down" but your connection is fine, DNS is usually the reason. Here is how it works and how to make it reliable.

Your Wi‑Fi icon shows full bars, the router's lights are green, yet no website loads. Video calls that are already running keep working. A few minutes later, everything is back. Moments like this often have nothing to do with your line. They come from DNS, the service that turns names into addresses, and most people never notice it until it fails.

The good news: DNS is easy to understand, and a few settings make it faster, more private and more robust.

What DNS Actually Does

Computers on the internet find each other by IP address, such as 203.0.113.7 or 2001:db8::7. People prefer names like example.com. The Domain Name System translates between the two, much like the contacts app on your phone: you tap a name, the phone dials the number.

Every time you open a website, send an email or a smart speaker checks for updates, a device first asks: what is the address for this name?

  1. Your device asks a resolver. In most homes this is the router, which passes the question on to the provider's DNS servers. The router hands that setting to every device when it joins, as described in IP addresses, DHCP and NAT.
  2. The resolver finds the answer. If it does not already know it, it works its way down from the root servers to the servers responsible for .com and finally to the server responsible for example.com itself. It is a small tour of the internet's backbone.
  3. The answer is cached. Every answer comes with a lifetime, the TTL (time to live). Until it expires, your device, your router and the resolver reuse it instead of asking again.

That caching is why DNS is usually fast and why changes do not show up everywhere at once.

How DNS Problems Look

DNS failures have a recognisable pattern:

  • "Connected, no internet." Apps that already have a connection keep working, new connections fail.
  • Some sites load, others do not. Cached names still work, new ones fail.
  • Everything is slow to start, then fine. The first lookup of each name takes seconds instead of milliseconds.
  • A website moved and only some people see the new one. Old answers are still cached somewhere.

Quick test: Open a terminal and run nslookup example.com. If you get an address back quickly, DNS works. If it times out while a ping to 1.1.1.1 succeeds, your connection is fine and DNS is the problem.

Choosing a Resolver

By default you use your provider's resolver. That works, but it is not your only option:

Resolver Strengths Verdict
Your provider (default) Nothing to configure, often close to you on the network Fine as long as it is reliable; quality varies
Cloudflare 1.1.1.1 Very fast, strong privacy commitments A good general choice
Quad9 9.9.9.9 Run by a Swiss foundation, blocks known malicious domains Excellent default for families and small offices
Google 8.8.8.8 Fast and widely available Reliable, but one more service tied to Google
DNS4EU EU‑backed, servers within the EU, optional filtering Worth considering if you want European operators

Change the resolver on the router, not on every device. Then every device in the house benefits, including the television and the printer you would never configure by hand. Always enter two servers, so one can fail without taking you offline.

Encrypted DNS

Classic DNS travels unencrypted. Anyone on the path, such as a public hotspot operator, can read which names you look up and, in theory, tamper with the answers. Two standards fix this:

  • DNS over HTTPS (DoH) wraps the queries in normal web traffic. Firefox, Chrome and Edge support it in their settings.
  • DNS over TLS (DoT) uses its own encrypted channel. Android calls it Private DNS: under Settings → Network → Private DNS you can enter a hostname such as dns.quad9.net.

Encryption hides your lookups from the network, not from the resolver itself. Pick a resolver you trust, then encrypt the path to it.

Filtering: Fewer Ads, Fewer Threats

Because every connection starts with a lookup, DNS is a convenient place to block things:

  • Protective resolvers like Quad9 or filtered DNS4EU block known malware and phishing domains.
  • Your own filter such as Pi‑hole or AdGuard Home runs on a small computer at home, blocks ads and trackers for every device and shows you what your devices are talking to. Smart TVs are often surprisingly chatty. A small home server is all it needs.
  • Hosted filters such as NextDNS offer the same without hardware, including per‑device rules and parental controls.

Worth knowing: If you run your own DNS filter, it becomes critical infrastructure. When the little computer goes offline, so does everyone's internet. Configure a second resolver or keep the router able to fall back.

Names Inside Your Own Network

Devices on your network also need names: the printer, the NAS, the camera. A few rules avoid trouble:

  • Do not invent your own top‑level domain. Names ending in .local are reserved for automatic discovery (mDNS) and clash with it. For homes, home.arpa is the officially reserved choice.
  • Businesses should use a subdomain of a domain they own, for example intern.example.at. It is unambiguous, and certificates can be issued for it.
  • Give servers and printers fixed addresses (a DHCP reservation in the router), so names keep pointing to the right device.

Your Own Domain Is Infrastructure Too

For a business, the domain is the foundation of website, email and often logins. Losing control of it is one of the most damaging outages there is, and one of the easiest to prevent. The short version is below; the full checklist is in who really owns your domain:

  • Turn on auto‑renewal and keep the payment method current. Expired domains are a classic, entirely avoidable outage.
  • Protect the registrar account with two‑factor authentication. Whoever controls it controls your email.
  • Enable DNSSEC if your registrar supports it. It lets resolvers verify that answers really come from you.
  • Publish SPF, DKIM and DMARC records so others can tell your real emails from forged ones.
  • Lower the TTL a day before a migration (for example from 24 hours to 5 minutes). The switch then takes effect within minutes instead of a day.

When You Changed Something and Nothing Happens

Old answers live in several caches. Clear them from the inside out:

  1. The browser: restart it, or open a private window.
  2. The device: on Windows, ipconfig /flushdns; on a Mac, restarting is the simplest option.
  3. The router: a restart clears its cache.

If it still shows the old result, the remaining cache is at the resolver. There, only waiting for the TTL helps.

Conclusion

DNS is the step before every connection, which makes it both a single point of failure and a powerful lever. Set a reliable resolver on the router with a second one as backup, encrypt it where you can, and consider filtering for malware. Businesses should treat their domain like a key to the building: renewed, protected and documented. Most "the internet is broken" moments then turn into non‑events.
