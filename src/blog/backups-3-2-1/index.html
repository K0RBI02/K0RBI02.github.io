The 3‑2‑1 Rule: Backups That Survive Real Life

Theft, ransomware, a dropped laptop. A simple rule decides whether you lose an afternoon or years of work.

Almost everyone believes they have a backup. The photos are "in the cloud", the documents are "on OneDrive", the business data is "on the server with two disks". Then a laptop gets stolen, ransomware encrypts the shared drive, or someone deletes a folder that the sync dutifully deletes everywhere. Only then does it become clear which of these copies was really a backup.

The good news: a reliable backup is not complicated. It follows one rule that fits on a sticky note.

The Rule

3 copies of your data, on 2 different kinds of storage, with 1 copy somewhere else.

  • Three copies: the original plus two backups. A single backup fails exactly when you need it often enough to matter.
  • Two kinds of storage: for example the laptop's internal drive and an external disk, or a NAS and a cloud service. Different media rarely fail at the same time.
  • One off‑site: fire, water damage and burglary take everything in the same building with them.

Professionals extend this to 3‑2‑1‑1‑0: one copy that is offline or immutable, which ransomware cannot reach, and zero errors when you test a restore.

What Is Not a Backup

Three things feel like backups but protect you from much less than you think:

Sync Services

OneDrive, iCloud Drive, Google Drive and Dropbox synchronise. When a file is deleted or encrypted on one device, the change reaches every other device within seconds. Version history and recycle bins help, but they are limited in time (often around 30 days) and cleaning up after ransomware file by file is tedious. Sync is excellent for working across devices, not for recovering from disasters.

RAID and Mirrored Disks

A NAS or server with two mirrored disks keeps running when one disk dies. That is about availability, not backup. A deleted file is deleted on both disks, and ransomware encrypts both.

The common RAID levels in one sentence each: RAID 0 spreads data across disks for speed and has no redundancy at all; RAID 1 mirrors two disks; RAID 5 spreads data plus parity across three or more disks and survives one failure; RAID 10 stripes across mirrored pairs for speed and redundancy. None of them keeps old versions of your files. More in RAID and NAS: What Disk Arrays Really Protect You From.

A Disk That Is Always Connected

An external drive that stays plugged in is reachable by anything that runs on the computer, including ransomware. Plug it in for the backup, unplug it afterwards, or use a backup target that keeps protected versions.

Rule of thumb: If one mistake, one stolen bag or one piece of malware can destroy the original and the copy, it is not a backup.

A Practical Setup at Home

For most households, three building blocks are enough:

  1. Automatic local backup. Use File History on Windows or Time Machine on a Mac with an external drive or a NAS. It runs in the background and keeps older versions.
  2. Cloud backup for the off‑site copy. A real backup service (not just a sync folder) copies everything encrypted to a data centre and keeps deleted files for months.
  3. Phones count too. Turn on the photo and device backup on your phone. That is where most irreplaceable memories live today.

A simple alternative to the cloud: two external drives that you swap every month, one of which always stays with relatives or at work.

A Practical Setup for Small Businesses

In a business, the stakes and the attack surface are higher. These points make the difference:

  • Back up your cloud services. Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace keep your data available, but their retention is not a backup. Deleted mailboxes, accidental mass deletions and compromised accounts are your responsibility. Dedicated backup tools close this gap.
  • Keep at least one copy immutable. Many NAS systems and cloud storage services offer snapshots or object lock: once written, a backup cannot be changed or deleted for a set period, not even by an administrator account.
  • Separate the credentials. The backup system should not use the same login as the everyday admin account, and its console needs two‑factor authentication. Attackers look for backups first.
  • Encrypt backups that leave the building. They contain the same personal data as the original, with the same data protection obligations. Device encryption explains the options and the recovery keys.
  • Know your retention duties. In Austria, tax‑relevant records generally have to be kept for seven years, so archives need to last that long and stay readable.

Two Questions to Answer in Advance

Before setting anything up, decide two things with whoever relies on the data:

Question In plain words Typical answer
How much data can we afford to lose? How far back can the last backup be? Home: a day. Office: an hour to a day
How long can we be without it? How quickly must everything run again? Home: a weekend. Office: hours, not days

The answers decide how often backups run and whether a slow restore from the cloud is acceptable, or whether you need a local copy that can be restored quickly.

Test the Restore, Not the Backup

A green tick in the backup software only means that something was copied. Whether you can get your data back is a different question: A heartbeat check warns you the day a backup stops running.

  • Restore a single file every month, ideally an important one.
  • Restore a whole system once a year, for example onto a spare disk or a test computer.
  • Write down how it works. In an emergency, the person who set it up may not be available, and stress is a bad time to learn.

Conclusion

Backups fail in predictable ways: one copy only, everything in one place, always connected, never tested. The 3‑2‑1 rule avoids all four. Start with an automatic local backup and an off‑site copy, make one of them unreachable for ransomware, and restore something today. If it works, you have a backup. If not, you found out at the best possible moment.
