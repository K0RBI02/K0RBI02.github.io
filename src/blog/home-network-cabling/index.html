One Cable Beats Any Gadget: Network Cabling at Home

Building, renovating or just fed up with weak Wi‑Fi upstairs? A few cables in the right places solve problems no repeater can.

Every home network starts with one line coming into the building. From there, most homes rely on a single router and the hope that its radio reaches every room. When it does not, repeaters, mesh units and powerline adapters follow. Each of them helps a little and costs a lot of money.

Experienced network installers usually suggest something else first: a cable. It is not exciting, but nothing else delivers full speed, stable latency and power for an access point at the same time.

Why Cable Wins

A network cable does a few things radio cannot:

  • Full speed in both directions at once. Wi‑Fi devices take turns on the same channel; a cable gives every device its own lane.
  • Stable latency. Video calls, gaming and remote desktops suffer more from jitter than from low bandwidth. On a cable, latency barely moves.
  • No neighbours. In an apartment building, a cable does not care how many networks share the air.
  • Power over the same line. With Power over Ethernet (PoE), a single cable powers and connects an access point or camera. The standards these access points speak are explained in Wi‑Fi 6, 6E and 7.

Worth knowing: The best Wi‑Fi is cabled Wi‑Fi. One access point per floor, each connected by cable, outperforms almost every purely wireless mesh system. The other usual suspects are in why your Wi‑Fi is slow.

Which Cable to Use

Network cables are sorted into categories. For new installations, the choice is simple:

Category Speed and distance Verdict
Cat 5e 1 Gbit/s to 100 m, 2.5 Gbit/s usually fine Enough for today's devices, little headroom
Cat 6 10 Gbit/s up to about 55 m Good for most houses with short runs
Cat 6A 10 Gbit/s up to 100 m, better shielding The sensible standard for new installations
"Cat 7" installation cable Very good cable, usually terminated as Cat 6A Common in Austria and Germany; fine, but sockets are Cat 6A

The cable itself is cheap compared to the work of laying it. If walls are open anyway, use Cat 6A and do not save on the number of runs.

Plan a Star, Not a Chain

A good home network looks like a star: every cable runs from one central point to its room.

  1. Choose the central point. Usually where the internet line enters: the utility room, the basement, a cupboard in the hallway. It needs a power socket and a little ventilation.
  2. Install a patch panel and a switch there. The panel keeps the wall cables tidy; short patch cables connect them to the switch and the router.
  3. Run cables to every room that matters, and plan generously:
    • Living room: two ports at the TV spot; a small switch shares them out to more devices (see below).
    • Home office: at least two ports at the desk.
    • Bedrooms: one or two ports.
    • Ceiling or high on the wall in a central spot on every floor: one port for an access point.
  4. Add empty conduit. An empty pipe to every floor and to the attic lets you pull new cables in ten years, for example fibre.

Rule of thumb: A cable you do not need costs a few euros. A cable you need but did not lay costs a renovation.

Many Devices in One Spot: Cable or Switch?

The TV cabinet is the classic case: television, game console, media player and soundbar all want a connection. Instead of pulling four cables to the same spot, it is often smarter to lay one or two and put a small switch behind the cabinet that shares them out.

  • Lower cost and effort: fewer cables, sockets and patch panel ports, and less space in the conduit. A small unmanaged switch costs about as much as a single wall socket installed.
  • Usually no noticeable slowdown: the devices share the bandwidth of the cable, but a TV, a console and a soundbar rarely come close to one gigabit together.
  • Two cables instead of one leave a reserve: one can feed the switch, the other stays free for a device that should get its own line, or for a separate network such as the smart home.
  • The trade‑offs: one more device that needs power and can fail, and devices behind the same switch can only be separated into different networks with a managed switch.

Cost vs. benefit: For places with many devices and little traffic, one or two cables plus a switch is the sensible choice. Where every device needs full speed, such as a home office desk or a NAS, give each its own cable.

Without Opening the Walls

Not everyone is building or renovating. In existing homes and rented apartments, there are still options:

  • Flat cables fit under skirting boards, along door frames or under carpets. They are less robust, but often invisible.
  • Existing cables in the walls can sometimes be reused. Old telephone and TV cables can carry network traffic with adapters using the G.hn standard, often more reliably than the power wiring.
  • Powerline adapters use the electrical wiring. Results depend heavily on the building: test them before buying several, and keep them out of power strips.
  • A wireless bridge between two fixed points, for example to a garden office, works well with outdoor directional antennas.

Details That Save Trouble Later

  • Keep data and power cables apart where they run in parallel, and leave in‑wall work to a qualified electrician.
  • Label both ends of every cable, for example "OG‑Bad‑1" at the socket and on the patch panel.
  • Test every run with a simple cable tester before the walls are closed. Fixing a bad termination later is much harder.
  • Choose a PoE switch if you plan access points or cameras. It saves power supplies at awkward places on the ceiling.
  • Document the result. A photo of the open wall and a simple floor plan with the cable routes are invaluable later.

What It Costs

The material for a typical house is a few hundred euros: cable, sockets, a patch panel and a switch. The work is the bigger part, which is why cabling pays off most when walls or floors are open anyway. For an existing apartment, a single flat cable to the most important room is often the best value upgrade there is.

Conclusion

Wireless is convenient, but cable is reliable. If you are building or renovating, plan a star with Cat 6A, a central point and an access point per floor. In existing homes, one well‑placed cable or a G.hn adapter over existing wiring often solves what three repeaters could not. It is the least exciting part of a network, and the one you will be grateful for every day.
