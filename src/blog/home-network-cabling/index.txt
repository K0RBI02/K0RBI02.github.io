14:T1ed3,<p>Every home network starts with one line coming into the building. From there, most homes rely on a single router and the hope that its radio reaches every room. When it does not, repeaters, <a href="/src/blog/mesh-wifi-explained/">mesh units</a> and powerline adapters follow. Each of them helps a little and costs a lot of money.</p>
<p>Experienced network installers usually suggest something else first: a cable. It is not exciting, but nothing else delivers full speed, stable latency and power for an access point at the same time.</p>
<h2 id="why-cable-wins">Why Cable Wins</h2>
<p>A network cable does a few things radio cannot:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Full speed in both directions at once.</strong> Wi‑Fi devices take turns on the same channel; a cable gives every device its own lane.</li>
<li><strong>Stable latency.</strong> Video calls, gaming and remote desktops suffer more from jitter than from low bandwidth. On a cable, latency barely moves.</li>
<li><strong>No neighbours.</strong> In an apartment building, a cable does not care how many networks share the air.</li>
<li><strong>Power over the same line.</strong> With Power over Ethernet (PoE), a single cable powers and connects an access point or camera. The standards these access points speak are explained in <a href="/src/blog/wifi-6-6e-7-explained/">Wi‑Fi 6, 6E and 7</a>.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Worth knowing:</strong> The best Wi‑Fi is cabled Wi‑Fi. One access point per floor, each connected by cable, outperforms almost every purely wireless mesh system. The other usual suspects are in <a href="/src/blog/why-your-wifi-is-slow/">why your Wi‑Fi is slow</a>.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="which-cable-to-use">Which Cable to Use</h2>
<p>Network cables are sorted into categories. For new installations, the choice is simple:</p>
<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Category</th>
<th>Speed and distance</th>
<th>Verdict</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Cat 5e</td>
<td>1 Gbit/s to 100 m, 2.5 Gbit/s usually fine</td>
<td>Enough for today's devices, little headroom</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Cat 6</td>
<td>10 Gbit/s up to about 55 m</td>
<td>Good for most houses with short runs</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Cat 6A</td>
<td>10 Gbit/s up to 100 m, better shielding</td>
<td>The sensible standard for new installations</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>"Cat 7" installation cable</td>
<td>Very good cable, usually terminated as Cat 6A</td>
<td>Common in Austria and Germany; fine, but sockets are Cat 6A</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
<p>The cable itself is cheap compared to the work of laying it. If walls are open anyway, use Cat 6A and do not save on the number of runs.</p>
<h2 id="plan-a-star-not-a-chain">Plan a Star, Not a Chain</h2>
<p>A good home network looks like a star: every cable runs from one central point to its room.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Choose the central point.</strong> Usually where the internet line enters: the utility room, the basement, a cupboard in the hallway. It needs a power socket and a little ventilation.</li>
<li><strong>Install a patch panel and a switch there.</strong> The panel keeps the wall cables tidy; short patch cables connect them to the switch and the router.</li>
<li><strong>Run cables to every room that matters</strong>, and plan generously:
<ul>
<li><strong>Living room:</strong> two ports at the TV spot; a small switch shares them out to more devices (see below).</li>
<li><strong>Home office:</strong> at least two ports at the desk.</li>
<li><strong>Bedrooms:</strong> one or two ports.</li>
<li><strong>Ceiling or high on the wall in a central spot on every floor:</strong> one port for an access point.</li>
</ul>
</li>
<li><strong>Add empty conduit.</strong> An empty pipe to every floor and to the attic lets you pull new cables in ten years, for example fibre.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> A cable you do not need costs a few euros. A cable you need but did not lay costs a renovation.</p>
</blockquote>
<h3 id="many-devices-in-one-spot-cable-or-switch">Many Devices in One Spot: Cable or Switch?</h3>
<p>The TV cabinet is the classic case: television, game console, media player and soundbar all want a connection. Instead of pulling four cables to the same spot, it is often smarter to lay <strong>one or two</strong> and put a small switch behind the cabinet that shares them out.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Lower cost and effort:</strong> fewer cables, sockets and patch panel ports, and less space in the conduit. A small unmanaged switch costs about as much as a single wall socket installed.</li>
<li><strong>Usually no noticeable slowdown:</strong> the devices share the bandwidth of the cable, but a TV, a console and a soundbar rarely come close to one gigabit together.</li>
<li><strong>Two cables instead of one</strong> leave a reserve: one can feed the switch, the other stays free for a device that should get its own line, or for a separate network such as the smart home.</li>
<li><strong>The trade‑offs:</strong> one more device that needs power and can fail, and devices behind the same switch can only be separated into different networks with a managed switch.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Cost vs. benefit:</strong> For places with many devices and little traffic, one or two cables plus a switch is the sensible choice. Where every device needs full speed, such as a home office desk or a NAS, give each its own cable.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="without-opening-the-walls">Without Opening the Walls</h2>
<p>Not everyone is building or renovating. In existing homes and rented apartments, there are still options:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Flat cables</strong> fit under skirting boards, along door frames or under carpets. They are less robust, but often invisible.</li>
<li><strong>Existing cables in the walls</strong> can sometimes be reused. Old telephone and TV cables can carry network traffic with adapters using the <em>G.hn</em> standard, often more reliably than the power wiring.</li>
<li><strong>Powerline adapters</strong> use the electrical wiring. Results depend heavily on the building: test them before buying several, and keep them out of power strips.</li>
<li><strong>A wireless bridge</strong> between two fixed points, for example to a garden office, works well with outdoor directional antennas.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="details-that-save-trouble-later">Details That Save Trouble Later</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Keep data and power cables apart</strong> where they run in parallel, and leave in‑wall work to a qualified electrician.</li>
<li><strong>Label both ends</strong> of every cable, for example "OG‑Bad‑1" at the socket and on the patch panel.</li>
<li><strong>Test every run</strong> with a simple cable tester before the walls are closed. Fixing a bad termination later is much harder.</li>
<li><strong>Choose a PoE switch</strong> if you plan access points or cameras. It saves power supplies at awkward places on the ceiling.</li>
<li><strong>Document the result.</strong> A photo of the open wall and a simple floor plan with the cable routes are invaluable later.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="what-it-costs">What It Costs</h2>
<p>The material for a typical house is a few hundred euros: cable, sockets, a patch panel and a switch. The work is the bigger part, which is why cabling pays off most when walls or floors are open anyway. For an existing apartment, a single flat cable to the most important room is often the best value upgrade there is.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Wireless is convenient, but cable is reliable. If you are building or renovating, plan a star with Cat 6A, a central point and an access point per floor. In existing homes, one well‑placed cable or a G.hn adapter over existing wiring often solves what three repeaters could not.
