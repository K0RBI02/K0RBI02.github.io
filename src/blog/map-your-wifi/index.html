Map Your Own Wi‑Fi: Find the Dead Spots With Nothing but Your Phone

Walk through your home, measure every room and fix what the numbers show, with a before‑and‑after map to prove it worked.

Everyone at home knows the spot. The video call freezes in the bedroom, the tablet gives up halfway down the hallway, and on the balcony the phone quietly drops back to mobile data. So the router gets restarted, a repeater goes on the shopping list, and nobody really knows whether any of it will help, because nobody has ever seen what the Wi‑Fi actually looks like.

The good news: you can see it, and you need nothing more than your phone, a sheet of paper and about an hour. This guide walks you through drawing your own Wi‑Fi map, fixing what it shows and measuring again, so you buy new hardware only if the numbers say you need it.

Why Measure at All

Our article on why your Wi‑Fi is slow explains the usual suspects: distance, walls, the wrong band and crowded channels. The trouble is that they all feel the same from the sofa. A map tells them apart, and each one has a different fix:

  • The signal tells you whether the radio reaches the room at all.
  • The speed tells you what actually arrives there.
  • The ping tells you how quickly and how steadily the network answers.

Together they stop you from guessing, and they give you something most Wi‑Fi tinkering never has: proof of whether a change helped.

What You Need

  • Your phone, with a Wi‑Fi analyser app. On Android, WiFiAnalyzer (open‑source) (opens in a new tab) is one example; it shows nearby networks, their channels and their signal strength.
  • A speed test. We use Speedtest by Ookla, as an app or at speedtest.net (opens in a new tab). Any other test works, as long as you stick to it.
  • A floor plan. A rough sketch with one box per room is enough.
  • A laptop and a network cable for the first measurement, if you have them.
  • The login for your router, for Step 5.

Worth knowing: iPhones don't allow classic analyser apps. Apple's free AirPort Utility has a hidden Wi‑Fi scanner that you switch on under the app's entry in the iOS Settings. It shows signal strength and channels as a plain list rather than a graph, which is enough for this guide.

1Step 1: Measure Your Baseline

Before you walk anywhere, find out what your line delivers, or you will blame the Wi‑Fi for something it can't fix.

  1. Connect the laptop to the router with a network cable and run the speed test.
  2. Run the same test on your phone, about a metre from the router.
  3. Write down download, upload and ping for both.

If the cable result is already far below your contract, stop here: that is a matter for your provider, and testing your line properly is your next read. If the cable is fine but the phone next to the router is poor, keep an eye on Step 5.

To make your numbers comparable, three rules apply from now on:

  • Keep it the same. Same phone, same app or website and, if you can choose it, the same test server.
  • Test more than once. Two or three runs per spot tell you more than one lucky result.
  • Close the rest. Pause cloud uploads and big downloads while you measure.

2Step 2: Draw Your Wi‑Fi Map

Now the walk. Mark one measuring point per room on your sketch, in the place where you actually use the Wi‑Fi: the sofa, the desk, the bed. Mark the router as well. Then, at each point:

  1. Stand still for a moment and let the analyser settle.
  2. Note the signal of your own network in dBm, and which band the phone is on.
  3. Run the speed test.
  4. Write everything down.

A simple list is all it takes:

Room / spot:
Time:
Signal (dBm):
Band (2.4 / 5 / 6 GHz):
Download / upload (Mbit/s):
Ping (ms):

Signal strength is shown as a negative number, and the closer it is to zero, the better: −50 is stronger than −70. These values are common rules of thumb from network vendors, not a standard:

  • Better than −60 dBm: very good.
  • −60 to −67 dBm: good, and fine for video calls.
  • −67 to −75 dBm: fine for browsing, but calls may start to stutter.
  • Worse than −75 dBm: expect trouble.

Colour your points in green, yellow and red, and the map starts talking. Repeat the walk once in the evening, when your neighbours are online too. A flat that looks fine at noon can look quite different at nine.

Rule of thumb: Compare only numbers from the same phone. Devices have different antennas, so −65 on one phone can be −60 on another.

Quick win: If the analyser seems to freeze, Android is limiting how often apps may scan. Waiting 30 seconds usually does it. Many phones can also switch this off under Developer options → Networking → Wi‑Fi scan throttling.

There is one limit to keep in mind. The speed test runs through your internet line, so it can never show more than the line delivers. On a slow line, every room may show the same speed even though the Wi‑Fi gets weaker, and then the signal and the ping tell you more.

3Step 3: Look at the Neighbours

Stand in your worst room and open the channel view of the analyser. Take a screenshot for 2.4 GHz and one for 5 GHz. You are looking for two things:

  • How crowded is your channel? Count the networks sharing it or overlapping it.
  • How loud are they? A neighbour at −85 dBm barely matters. One at −55 dBm is practically in the room with you.

Don't change anything yet. Placement comes first, because it often solves more than the channel does, and because it changes what your map will look like.

4Step 4: Improve the Placement

Moving the router costs nothing and is the single biggest lever you have. Radio spreads out in all directions, so a router in a corner sends half of its signal into the neighbour's flat.

  1. Free it. Out of the cupboard, away from the TV unit, away from metal, mirrors and aquariums.
  2. Lift it. A shelf at chest height or above beats the floor.
  3. Move it towards the middle. As far as the cabling allows.
  4. Check the antennas, if it has any. Vertical is a sensible starting point.

The router often has to stay near the socket where your line comes in. If your modem and router are separate devices, a longer network cable between them solves that. If they are one box, a network cable to a second access point may be the better route, and Step 7 will tell you.

After each change, repeat the measurement in your worst room and in one good room. Otherwise you may fix the bedroom and quietly ruin the kitchen.

5Step 5: Check Channels and Width

Now log in to your router. Remember: one change at a time, with a measurement after each, or you will never know what helped.

2.4 GHz

Use channel 1, 6 or 11, whichever is quietest in your screenshot, and keep the channel width at 20 MHz. Wider channels on this band mostly create more collisions. If you run a smart home, keep Zigbee and Wi‑Fi out of each other's way.

5 GHz

Here 80 MHz is a solid default; 160 MHz only pays off when the air around you is quiet. Channels 36 to 48 are the simple choice. Higher channels are often emptier, but many of them are shared with radar under the rules known as DFS: if the router detects a radar signal, it has to leave the channel, and your Wi‑Fi can drop out for a moment.

Worth knowing: Automatic channel selection is not the enemy. Modern routers often handle it well, and a fixed channel can't react when a neighbour moves onto it. If a manual channel brings no measurable gain, go back to automatic.

While you are in the settings, install any pending firmware update and work through the router checklist.

6Step 6: Measure Again

Take a clean sheet and repeat Step 2: same points, same phone, same test, and again in the evening. Then put the two maps next to each other. This is the most satisfying part of the whole job, and the only way to be sure the evening was well spent.

Tested by Korbi: before and after
  • Room: the living room, with the signal passing through a whole floor
  • Before: 45 Mbit/s down, 10 Mbit/s up
  • After moving one channel up and slightly changing the router's direction: 56.2 Mbit/s down, 12.3 Mbit/s up

A whole floor is one of the hardest paths for Wi‑Fi, because floors absorb more signal than walls. A small change can win back a fair amount, but placement only goes so far. If a room on another floor needs more, this is where a mesh system could help: with a node on the landing or on that floor, your devices no longer have to reach through the ceiling on their own.

7Step 7: Decide What Comes Next

With two maps on the table, the decision is usually easy:

What the map shows Likely cause What to do Verdict
Green everywhere you use it Nothing wrong Enjoy it Keep your money
One or two red rooms, the rest fine Distance and walls Add a radio, ideally with a cable Worth it, and the map shows where
Strong signal, poor speed, worse in the evening Crowded channels Try another channel or band Often free to fix
Fine on the phone, poor on one device The device Check its band, update it or replace it Don't rebuild the network for it
Every spot equally slow, cable too The line Test the line Talk to your provider
Good numbers, calls still stutter under load Bufferbloat Read about video calls and bufferbloat A queue problem, not a coverage one

If you do need more radios, mesh Wi‑Fi explained covers the options, and your map gives you the most important input: put a node where the signal is still good, not in the red room itself.

Try It on Screen First

Before you buy anything, you can test your idea in a planning tool. UniFi Design Center (opens in a new tab) from Ubiquiti is a free example that runs in the browser: you upload your floor plan, set the scale, draw the walls, place access points and get a colour map of the expected coverage. TP‑Link's Omada Design Hub does something similar. Both are built around their maker's own hardware, so treat them as a way to compare positions rather than as a buying list. Your router from another brand won't be in UniFi's catalogue, but you can adjust a device in the tool until it roughly resembles yours.

The key difference from your walk through the flat: these tools simulate, they don't measure. The result is only as good as the walls and materials you enter. That makes your measured map the perfect reality check: if the simulation shows green where you measured red, adjust the walls before you trust its other predictions.

For Small Businesses

The method works in an office too, with two changes: measure during working hours, when people, laptops and phones are all in the building, and include the spots that really matter, such as the meeting room and the reception desk. A shop or office that depends on Wi‑Fi usually wants cabled access points anyway. Above a few rooms, a professional site survey with proper tools is money well spent, and your own map is a good thing to bring to that conversation.

Conclusion

The bedroom where calls freeze doesn't have to stay a mystery. Check your line with a cable, walk through your home with your phone and write down signal, speed and ping for every room. Improve the placement first, then the channels, one change at a time, and draw the map again. Whatever is still red afterwards tells you exactly what to do next, and whether it is worth spending any money at all.
