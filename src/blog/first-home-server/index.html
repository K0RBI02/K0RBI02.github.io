Your First Home Server: Small Box, Big Difference

A Raspberry Pi or a mini PC can block ads, store photos and run your smart home. Here is what is worth running and how to keep it safe.

At some point, many people with a home network want a machine of their own: something that runs around the clock, keeps photos away from big cloud services, filters ads for the whole house or runs the smart home without depending on the internet. That sounds like a server room, but it fits on a bookshelf and uses less power than a light bulb.

The good news: getting started has never been easier, and a few rules keep it from turning into a liability.

Which Hardware?

Option Power at idle Verdict
Raspberry Pi 5 A few watts Great for one or two light services; use an SSD, not just a memory card
Used mini PC (office models) Around 5–15 W The best value: more power, real SSDs, room for many services
NAS with app support 15–40 W Convenient if you already have one; limited by its processor
Old desktop or laptop 20–60 W or more Free to start, but expensive in electricity over a year

A device drawing 10 W around the clock uses about 88 kWh a year, roughly the cost of one or two pizzas. An old desktop at 60 W costs six times as much.

What Is Worth Running

  • Network‑wide ad and tracker blocking with Pi‑hole or AdGuard Home. Usually the first service people install, see DNS explained.
  • Smart home hub with Home Assistant, which keeps automations working without any cloud (more on smart homes).
  • Photo library with Immich or a similar app, as a private alternative to cloud photo services.
  • Files and calendars with Nextcloud.
  • Media server with Jellyfin for your own films and music.
  • Backup target for the family laptops.

How to Run It: Containers

Most of these services are distributed as containers: each app comes packaged with everything it needs and runs isolated from the others. With Docker and a simple configuration file per service, installing, updating or removing an app takes minutes and leaves no mess behind. VMs and containers explains the idea in more detail.

Rules That Keep It Safe

  • Do not expose it to the internet. Reach it from outside through a VPN such as WireGuard or Tailscale, not through open ports. See reaching your network safely.
  • Update automatically, both the operating system and the containers.
  • Back it up. A home server easily becomes the only place where something lives. Treat it like any other device in the 3‑2‑1 rule.
  • Give it a fixed address in the router (a DHCP reservation), so other devices always find it.
  • Plan for failure. If the server runs DNS for the whole house and goes down, nobody has internet. Configure a fallback.

Start Small

The most common mistake is installing twenty services in the first weekend and maintaining none of them. Start with one or two that solve a real problem, run them for a month, and add more once you trust your setup and your backups.

Conclusion

A small, efficient computer at home can block ads for every device, keep your photos private and make the smart home independent of the cloud. A used mini PC is the sweet spot, containers keep the apps tidy, and three rules keep it safe: no open ports, automatic updates and a real backup. Start with one service and grow from there.
