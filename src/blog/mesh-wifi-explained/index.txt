15:T246c,<p>The router sits in the hallway, the Wi‑Fi is fine in the living room, and the moment you walk upstairs the video call freezes. You bought a repeater, which gave you a second network called <em>Home_EXT</em>, and your phone stubbornly holds on to the weak one until you switch manually. Mesh Wi‑Fi was invented for exactly this house.</p>
<p>The good news: a well‑placed mesh system makes several radios behave like one network, and you do not need to understand radio engineering to set it up properly.</p>
<h2 id="what-mesh-actually-means">What "Mesh" Actually Means</h2>
<p>A mesh system is a set of Wi‑Fi units, usually called <strong>nodes</strong>, that are managed together. One of them is connected to your internet router (or is the router), the others are spread across the home. Three things set it apart from a plain repeater:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>One network name.</strong> Every node sends the same name and password. Your devices see one network, not three.</li>
<li><strong>Central management.</strong> You configure the system once, in one app or admin page. Updates, settings and the guest network apply to every node.</li>
<li><strong>Coordinated roaming.</strong> The nodes know about each other and help your devices move to the closest one as you walk through the house.</li>
</ul>
<p>The word <em>mesh</em> suggests that every node talks to every other, like the Zigbee and Thread networks in a smart home. In practice, most home Wi‑Fi systems form a small tree: each satellite finds the best path back to the main node, sometimes via another satellite. What matters is not the shape, but how good each link is.</p>
<h2 id="the-backhaul-decides-everything">The Backhaul Decides Everything</h2>
<p>Each satellite has two jobs: talking to your devices, and passing their traffic back to the main node. That second connection is called the <strong>backhaul</strong>, and it is where mesh systems succeed or disappoint.</p>
<h3 id="wireless-backhaul-on-a-shared-band">Wireless Backhaul on a Shared Band</h3>
<p>The cheapest systems use the same radio for both jobs. Every packet travels through the air twice: from your laptop to the satellite, then from the satellite to the main node. Because a radio can only send or receive one thing at a time on a channel, the available speed is roughly halved at every hop. Two hops, and a quarter is left.</p>
<h3 id="dedicated-wireless-backhaul">Dedicated Wireless Backhaul</h3>
<p><strong>Tri‑band</strong> systems add a second 5 GHz or a 6 GHz radio used only for the link between the nodes. Your devices get one band, the backhaul gets its own. This is a real improvement and the minimum worth buying for a home where speed matters. Wi‑Fi 7 systems go further with <em>multi‑link operation</em>, which can spread the backhaul across several bands at once (see <a href="/src/blog/wifi-6-6e-7-explained/">Wi‑Fi 6, 6E and 7 explained</a>).</p>
<h3 id="wired-backhaul">Wired Backhaul</h3>
<p>If a network cable reaches the satellite, the radio is free to serve devices only. Each node then delivers full speed to its area, latency stays low, and the system no longer depends on how well the radio gets through a concrete ceiling. Most mesh systems support this, and many switch to the cable automatically as soon as you plug it in.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> Mesh with a cable to every node is the gold standard. Mesh without cable is a compromise, and a dedicated backhaul band is what makes that compromise acceptable.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>Even one cable helps. If you can reach the upper floor through an existing conduit or along a stairwell, connect that node and let the others link wirelessly. Our guide to <a href="/src/blog/home-network-cabling/">cabling your home network</a> shows ways to get there without opening walls.</p>

<h2 id="where-to-put-the-nodes">Where to Put the Nodes</h2>
<p>Placement matters more than the model. A satellite can only pass on what it receives, so it belongs where the signal from the main node is <strong>still good</strong>, not in the room where it is already gone.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Start from the main node.</strong> Put it as central and as high as your internet connection allows, out of cupboards and away from the floor.</li>
<li><strong>Place the first satellite halfway</strong> to the problem area, typically one room or one floor away, with as few walls in between as possible.</li>
<li><strong>Check the link quality.</strong> Most apps show it as a colour or a rating per node. If it says <em>weak</em>, move the satellite one room closer.</li>
<li><strong>Add more nodes only if needed.</strong> Every additional wireless node adds radio traffic. Two well‑placed units often beat four scattered ones.</li>
<li><strong>Test where it matters.</strong> Run a speed test on the sofa, at the desk and in the bedroom. The method from <a href="/src/blog/why-your-wifi-is-slow/">why your Wi‑Fi is slow</a> applies here too.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Quick win:</strong> On a staircase house, put the satellite on the landing or directly above the main node rather than in the far corner of the upper floor. Floors absorb more signal than walls.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="settings-worth-knowing">Settings Worth Knowing</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Roaming standards:</strong> Look for support of <strong>802.11k, v and r</strong>. They let nodes tell your devices which neighbour is better and make the handover fast enough that calls survive it. Most systems enable them by default; some hide 802.11r behind an option because a few old devices dislike it.</li>
<li><strong>One name for all bands:</strong> Keep band steering on, so devices are guided to 5 or 6 GHz when they are close and fall back to 2.4 GHz only when they need the range.</li>
<li><strong>Router mode or access point mode:</strong> If your provider's router stays in place, run the mesh system in <em>access point</em> or <em>bridge</em> mode. Otherwise you end up with two routers, two address ranges and devices that cannot find each other.</li>
<li><strong>Security:</strong> WPA3 or WPA2/WPA3 mixed, automatic updates on, admin access from the internet off. The <a href="/src/blog/router-hardening/">router security checklist</a> applies to the mesh system just as much.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Worth knowing:</strong> Mixing brands rarely works well. The Wi‑Fi Alliance standard <em>EasyMesh</em> is meant to let units from different manufacturers cooperate, but support varies widely. Plan on staying with one manufacturer, or check explicitly that your router and the satellites are listed as compatible. Some routers common in Austria, such as the FRITZ!Box, have their own mesh feature that works best with repeaters from the same maker.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="when-mesh-is-not-the-answer">When Mesh Is Not the Answer</h2>
<p>Mesh solves coverage. It does not fix a slow internet line, an overloaded old router or a building where every channel is crowded by the neighbours. If a cabled laptop next to the router already gets poor results, start with <a href="/src/blog/fiber-cable-dsl-5g/">the line itself</a>. And in a small flat, a single good router in the right place usually beats any mesh set.</p>
<h2 id="for-small-businesses">For Small Businesses</h2>
<p>Consumer mesh systems work well in a small office, but a few points change:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prefer cabled access points.</strong> Offices are often easier to cable than homes, and ceiling‑mounted access points with a wired connection are more predictable than satellites on shelves.</li>
<li><strong>Look for VLAN support</strong> so staff, guests and devices such as printers can live in <a href="/src/blog/vlans-managed-switches/">separate networks</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Avoid cloud‑only management</strong> you cannot export. If the manufacturer's account disappears or the person who set it up leaves, you still need access.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Mesh Wi‑Fi turns several radios into one network with one name, one app and smooth roaming. Whether it is also fast depends on the backhaul: a cable is best, a dedicated backhaul band is the minimum without one. Place satellites where the signal is still good, check the link quality, enable the roaming standards, and stay with one manufacturer.
