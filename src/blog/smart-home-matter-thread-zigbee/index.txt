15:T166c,<p>It starts with one smart plug and an app. A few years later there are bulbs, thermostats, door sensors and a robot vacuum, four apps, three hubs and a Wi‑Fi network full of devices that stop working whenever a manufacturer's cloud has a bad day. Smart homes rarely fail because of one device. They fail because of the way all of them are connected.</p>
<p>The good news: the industry has largely agreed on open standards, and with a bit of planning a smart home can be fast, local and independent of any single company.</p>
<h2 id="four-names-two-layers">Four Names, Two Layers</h2>
<p>The terms get mixed up constantly, because they describe different things:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>The radio layer</strong> decides <em>how</em> a device transmits: Wi‑Fi, Thread, Zigbee or Z‑Wave.</li>
<li><strong>The application layer</strong> decides <em>what</em> devices say to each other and which apps can control them. That is where Matter comes in.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="why-mesh-radios-help">Why Mesh Radios Help</h2>
<p>Zigbee, Z‑Wave and Thread form a <strong>mesh</strong>: mains‑powered devices such as bulbs and plugs pass messages on for others. The more devices you have, the better the coverage gets, the opposite of Wi‑Fi, where every additional device competes for airtime. Battery sensors can run for years because they only wake up briefly to send a message.</p>
<h2 id="matter-one-device-many-apps">Matter: One Device, Many Apps</h2>
<p>Matter's biggest benefit is <strong>multi‑admin</strong>: the same device can be controlled from Apple Home, Google Home, Amazon Alexa, SmartThings and Home Assistant at the same time. Matter devices talk to each other locally on your network, so a switch still turns on a light when the internet is down.</p>
<p>Matter devices over <strong>Thread</strong> need a <em>border router</em> that connects the Thread mesh to your home network. Many households already have one without knowing it: recent Apple TV and HomePod models, several Google Nest and Amazon Echo devices act as border routers. Ideally you have two, so the mesh does not depend on a single box.</p>
<h2 id="what-your-network-needs">What Your Network Needs</h2>
<p>Smart home protocols lean on parts of the network that people like to switch off:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Keep IPv6 enabled on your home network.</strong> Thread and Matter are built on IPv6. Even without IPv6 from your provider, the local addresses inside your network must work (<a href="/src/blog/ipv6-at-home-and-in-the-office/">more on IPv6</a>).</li>
<li><strong>Allow device discovery.</strong> Matter and many apps find devices via multicast (mDNS). If you separate smart home devices into <a href="/src/blog/segmenting-your-home-network/">their own network</a>, which is a good idea, your router or firewall needs to forward this discovery between the networks, often called an <a href="/src/blog/vlans-managed-switches/"><em>mDNS reflector</em></a> or <em>multicast proxy</em>.</li>
<li><strong>Mind the 2.4 GHz band.</strong> Wi‑Fi, Zigbee and Thread share it. If your Wi‑Fi uses channels 1, 6 and 11, Zigbee channels 15, 20 or 25 sit in the gaps between them.</li>
<li><strong>Give hubs a cable.</strong> Put Zigbee hubs and border routers on Ethernet where possible, and in a central spot, not in the cupboard behind the TV.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="local-first">Local First</h2>
<p>The most reliable smart home does not depend on the internet at all. Some practical rules:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Prefer devices that work locally</strong> via Matter, Zigbee or a local API. If a product needs an account and a cloud to switch a light, its lifetime ends when the company loses interest.</li>
<li><strong>Consider a local hub.</strong> <em>Home Assistant</em> runs on a <a href="/src/blog/first-home-server/">small computer at home</a>, supports almost every protocol and keeps automations running offline.</li>
<li><strong>Keep the existing hubs.</strong> A Philips Hue bridge or similar can expose its Zigbee devices to Matter, so you do not have to replace working hardware.</li>
<li><strong>Update firmware.</strong> Smart home devices are network devices with all the security implications, and updates fix both bugs and vulnerabilities.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>A smart home becomes reliable when the radio layer and the application layer are chosen deliberately: Thread or Zigbee for small battery devices, Wi‑Fi only for devices that need bandwidth, Matter as the common language and a local hub that works without the internet. Keep IPv6 and device discovery working, give the smart devices their own network and place hubs well.
