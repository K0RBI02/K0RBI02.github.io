15:T1980,<p>You pay for a fast connection, run a speed test on the sofa and get a fraction of it. The instinct is to call the provider. In most homes, though, the bottleneck is not the line coming into the building — it is the last few metres of radio between the router and your device.</p>
<p>The good news: those last few metres are the part you can actually fix.</p>
<h2 id="measure-before-you-change-anything">Measure Before You Change Anything</h2>
<p>Before you buy anything, find out where the speed gets lost.</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Wired test first.</strong> Plug a laptop into the router with a <a href="/src/blog/home-network-cabling/">network cable</a> and run a speed test. This is what your connection really delivers.</li>
<li><strong>Wireless test next to the router.</strong> Same device, same test, one metre from the router over Wi‑Fi.</li>
<li><strong>Wireless test where it hurts.</strong> The bedroom, the home office, the garden.</li>
</ol>
<p>If the wired result is already low, the problem is the line or the router — talk to your provider. If the wired result is fine but the wireless numbers drop sharply with distance, the problem is coverage. If even the test next to the router is poor, look at interference and settings.</p>
<h2 id="the-usual-suspects">The Usual Suspects</h2>
<h3 id="distance-and-walls">Distance and Walls</h3>
<p>Wi‑Fi is radio, and radio gets weaker with every wall, floor and piece of furniture. Reinforced concrete, underfloor heating, mirrors and large water-filled objects such as aquariums are especially hard on the signal. A router in the basement or inside a metal cabinet is fighting a losing battle.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Quick win:</strong> Move the router out of cupboards and corners and put it as high and central as the cabling allows. It costs nothing and often helps more than new hardware.</p>
</blockquote>
<h3 id="the-wrong-band">The Wrong Band</h3>
<p>Modern routers transmit on several frequency bands, and each has a trade-off:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>2.4 GHz</strong> reaches far and penetrates walls well, but it is slow and crowded. In most setups there are only three channels that do not overlap, shared with your neighbours, baby monitors and Bluetooth.</li>
<li><strong>5 GHz</strong> is much faster and has many more channels, but it fades faster through walls.</li>
<li><strong>6 GHz</strong> (<a href="/src/blog/wifi-6-6e-7-explained/">Wi‑Fi 6E and Wi‑Fi 7</a>) offers wide, clean channels with almost no interference — but it has the shortest range and needs devices that support it.</li>
</ul>
<p>A laptop that clings to a weak 2.4 GHz connection while a strong 5 GHz signal is available will feel slow. Using one network name for all bands ("band steering") usually helps devices pick the better one.</p>
<h3 id="crowded-channels">Crowded Channels</h3>
<p>In apartment buildings, dozens of networks compete for the same airtime. Most routers choose a channel automatically, but not always well. A free Wi‑Fi analyser app shows which channels are busy around you. On 2.4 GHz, stick to channels 1, 6 or 11 and keep the channel width at 20 MHz — wider channels on 2.4 GHz mostly create more interference.</p>
<h3 id="old-devices">Old Devices</h3>
<p>A network runs at the pace of the devices using it. An old phone or a cheap smart plug with weak radio takes longer to send the same amount of data, using up airtime the other devices then lack. Newer Wi‑Fi standards handle this better, which is one of the real benefits of upgrading.</p>
<h2 id="mesh-repeaters-or-access-points">Mesh, Repeaters or Access Points?</h2>
<p>When one router cannot cover the whole home, you need more radios. The options differ a lot in quality:</p>
<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Option</th>
<th>How it connects</th>
<th>Verdict</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Repeater</td>
<td>Wireless, repeats the signal</td>
<td>Cheap, but every hop costs speed and adds latency</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Mesh system</td>
<td>Wireless backhaul, coordinated nodes</td>
<td>Good roaming; much better with a dedicated backhaul band</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Mesh or access points with cable</td>
<td>Wired backhaul via Ethernet</td>
<td>Best performance — the gold standard</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Powerline adapters</td>
<td>Through the power wiring</td>
<td>Highly dependent on your wiring; test before you rely on it</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
<p>If there is any way to run a cable — even one through a ceiling to the upper floor — use it. A wired access point delivers full speed to its area, while a wireless hop always shares airtime between talking to the router and talking to your devices.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> Place mesh nodes where the signal is still good, not where it is already bad. A node can only pass on what it receives.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>How mesh systems work, and why the link between the nodes matters so much, is covered in <a href="/src/blog/mesh-wifi-explained/">mesh Wi‑Fi explained</a>.</p>
<h2 id="settings-worth-checking">Settings Worth Checking</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Firmware:</strong> Update the router. Providers and manufacturers regularly fix performance and security problems.</li>
<li><strong>Security mode:</strong> Use WPA3 or "WPA2/WPA3 mixed". Never use WEP or open networks. More settings are in the <a href="/src/blog/router-hardening/">router checklist</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Legacy modes:</strong> Disable very old Wi‑Fi standards (802.11b) if no device needs them.</li>
<li><strong>Channel width:</strong> 80 MHz on 5 GHz is a solid default; 160 MHz only helps when the air around you is quiet.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="when-it-really-is-the-line">When It Really Is the Line</h2>
<p>If the wired test next to the router is consistently below what you pay for — especially at the same times every evening — collect a few measurements with dates and times and send them to your provider. Numbers from a cable connection carry far more weight than a screenshot of a Wi‑Fi test from the garden. In Austria, the regulator's free certified measurement turns them into real evidence, as described in <a href="/src/blog/speed-test-and-your-contract/">speed tests done right</a>.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Slow Wi‑Fi is almost always a local problem: placement, bands, interference or coverage. Measure wired first, then wireless, then decide.
