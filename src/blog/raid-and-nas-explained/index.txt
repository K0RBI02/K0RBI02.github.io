15:T1ce1,<p>A NAS (network attached storage) is a small computer full of hard drives that the whole household or office can reach: shared folders, photo libraries, laptop backups, camera recordings. Almost every NAS asks the same question during setup: <em>which RAID level?</em> The answer decides how much space you get, how fast it is and what happens when a disk dies, which it eventually will.</p>
<p>The good news: for a home or small office, the choice comes down to a handful of options, and one of them is almost always right.</p>
<h2 id="what-raid-does">What RAID Does</h2>
<p>RAID (redundant array of independent disks) combines several drives into one volume. Depending on the level, it does one or both of two things:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Striping:</strong> data is split into blocks and spread across several disks, so they work in parallel. That makes it faster.</li>
<li><strong>Redundancy:</strong> extra copies or parity information let the array keep running when a disk fails. That makes it more available.</li>
</ul>
<p>What RAID never does is keep old versions of your files. If a file is deleted, overwritten or encrypted by ransomware, that change is written to every disk at once. RAID protects you from a broken disk, not from mistakes or attacks.</p>
<h2 id="the-four-classic-levels">The Four Classic Levels</h2>
<h3 id="raid-0-striping-no-safety-net">RAID 0: Striping, No Safety Net</h3>
<p>Two or more disks, data spread across all of them. You get the full capacity of all disks and the highest speed. But if <strong>any single disk</strong> fails, the data on <strong>all</strong> of them is lost, so the risk grows with every disk you add.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Verdict:</strong> Only for data you can throw away, such as a scratch disk for video editing. Never for anything you want to keep.</p>
</blockquote>
<h3 id="raid-1-the-mirror">RAID 1: The Mirror</h3>
<p>Two disks with identical content. One may fail completely and the other carries on as if nothing happened. You pay with capacity: two 8 TB disks give you 8 TB.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Verdict:</strong> The simple, robust choice for a two‑bay NAS. Rebuilding after a failure is just copying one disk to the new one.</p>
</blockquote>
<h3 id="raid-5-striping-with-parity">RAID 5: Striping With Parity</h3>
<p>At least three disks. Data is striped across them, plus <em>parity</em>: extra information from which any one missing disk can be recalculated. You lose the capacity of one disk: four 8 TB disks give you 24 TB.</p>
<p>The catch is the rebuild. After a failure, the array reads every remaining disk completely to recreate the missing one. With today's large disks this takes a day or more, stresses disks that are often the same age, and a second failure or a read error during that time can cost the whole array.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Verdict:</strong> Efficient with space, but with large disks consider RAID 6 instead (see below).</p>
</blockquote>
<h3 id="raid-10-mirrors-then-striping">RAID 10: Mirrors, Then Striping</h3>
<p>At least four disks, arranged as mirrored pairs that are then striped. You get half the raw capacity, very good performance and fast rebuilds, because only one mirror partner has to be copied. The array survives one failure per pair, though losing both disks of the same pair is fatal.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Verdict:</strong> The performance choice for four or more disks, for example for virtual machines or many simultaneous users.</p>
</blockquote>

<p>Two common extras: <strong>RAID 6</strong> works like RAID 5 with double parity and survives two failures, the safer choice for large disks. Many NAS systems also offer their own flexible variants, such as <strong>Synology Hybrid RAID (SHR)</strong>, which make better use of disks of different sizes and let you grow the array one disk at a time.</p>
<h2 id="choosing-for-a-home-or-small-office">Choosing for a Home or Small Office</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Two bays:</strong> RAID 1 (or SHR with two disks, which is the same thing).</li>
<li><strong>Four bays, mostly storage:</strong> RAID 6 or SHR‑2 if you want to sleep well, RAID 5 or SHR‑1 if capacity matters more.</li>
<li><strong>Four bays, heavy use:</strong> RAID 10.</li>
<li><strong>One bay:</strong> no RAID at all, which is fine as long as the backup is good.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="details-that-matter-more-than-the-level">Details That Matter More Than the Level</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Use drives made for NAS use.</strong> They are built for running around the clock. Avoid SMR drives (shingled recording) in RAID: their slow rewrites can make rebuilds take days or fail altogether.</li>
<li><strong>Turn on notifications.</strong> A RAID that has quietly been running on one disk for months has no redundancy left. Let the NAS send an email or push message when a disk shows errors. <a href="/src/blog/monitoring-small-networks/">Monitoring</a> can collect these warnings in one place.</li>
<li><strong>Run regular scrubs.</strong> A scheduled data scrub reads everything and repairs silent errors while all disks are still healthy.</li>
<li><strong>Use snapshots.</strong> File systems like Btrfs and ZFS can keep read‑only snapshots, for example every hour for a week. They are what actually rescues you after an accidental deletion or ransomware, as long as nobody can delete them.</li>
<li><strong>Do not expose the NAS directly to the internet.</strong> NAS systems with open ports have been hit by mass ransomware campaigns. Reach it through a VPN instead, as described in <a href="/src/blog/secure-remote-access/">Reaching Your Network Safely</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="raid-is-not-a-backup">RAID Is Not a Backup</h2>
<p>This is the point worth repeating. A NAS with RAID keeps working when a disk dies, which is valuable. But fire, theft, <a href="/src/blog/ups-for-your-network/">a power surge</a>, a broken controller, a deleted folder or ransomware can still take everything at once. The NAS is a great <em>place for one copy</em> of your data, ideally with snapshots. It still needs its own backup on another device and outside the building, as described in <a href="/src/blog/backups-3-2-1/">the 3‑2‑1 rule</a>.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>For most homes and small offices, the decision is short: RAID 1 for two disks, RAID 6 or SHR for four, RAID 10 when speed matters, RAID 0 only for data you can lose. Then turn on notifications, scrubs and snapshots, keep the NAS off the open internet, and back it up somewhere else. RAID buys you time when a disk fails.
