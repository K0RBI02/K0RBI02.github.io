15:T1695,<p>You tap a link on the sofa, and a fraction of a second later the page is there. Between those two moments, your request has left the house, crossed your provider's network, possibly an exchange point, and perhaps an ocean. It found its way through tens of thousands of independent networks without any central authority deciding the route.</p>
<p>This blog covers the living room and the server room. This article is about everything in between: the backbone.</p>
<h2 id="step-1-out-of-the-house">Step 1: Out of the House</h2>
<p>Your device sends the request over Wi‑Fi or cable to the router, which forwards it over the access line, fibre, cable, DSL or mobile, into your provider's network. The first decision has already happened before that: <a href="/src/blog/dns-explained/">DNS</a> turned the name into an address.</p>
<h2 id="step-2-a-network-of-networks">Step 2: A Network of Networks</h2>
<p>The internet is not one network. It is made of <strong>tens of thousands of independent networks</strong>, called <em>autonomous systems</em>: providers, universities, companies, content platforms, each with its own number. Your provider is one of them; the website you are visiting is in another.</p>
<p>To get from one to the other, networks connect in two ways:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Transit:</strong> a smaller network pays a larger one to carry its traffic to the rest of the internet.</li>
<li><strong>Peering:</strong> two networks exchange traffic between their own customers directly, often without payment, because both benefit.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="step-3-internet-exchange-points">Step 3: Internet Exchange Points</h2>
<p>Peering happens at <strong>internet exchange points (IXPs)</strong>: buildings where hundreds of networks place their equipment and connect to a shared switching platform. Instead of running a cable to every other network, each connects once to the exchange.</p>
<p>Austria's best‑known exchange is the <strong>VIX in Vienna</strong>; in Germany, <strong>DE‑CIX in Frankfurt</strong> is one of the largest exchanges in the world. When your Austrian provider and an Austrian website both connect to the same exchange, your click may never leave the country.</p>
<h2 id="step-4-across-the-ocean">Step 4: Across the Ocean</h2>
<p>When the destination really is far away, traffic travels through <strong>submarine cables</strong>: hundreds of them, together well over a million kilometres, lying on the sea floor between continents. Each holds a handful of fibre pairs, protected by layers of steel and plastic, with amplifiers every few dozen kilometres powered through the cable itself. Satellites carry only a small share of international traffic; the bulk crosses the oceans in glass.</p>
<p>Even light in glass has a speed limit, about 200,000 kilometres per second. A round trip from Vienna to New York and back therefore takes at least several dozen milliseconds, no matter how fast your own connection is.</p>
<h2 id="step-5-usually-it-never-gets-that-far">Step 5: Usually, It Never Gets That Far</h2>
<p>Most of what people use every day, streaming, software updates, large websites, comes from <strong>content delivery networks</strong>: copies of popular content stored in data centres close to the users, often directly inside the providers' own networks. Your evening series probably comes from a server a few dozen kilometres away, not from another continent. That is a big reason why the internet copes with today's traffic at all.</p>
<h2 id="how-the-route-is-chosen-bgp">How the Route Is Chosen: BGP</h2>
<p>Networks tell each other which addresses they can reach using the <strong>Border Gateway Protocol (BGP)</strong>. Every network announces "I can deliver to these addresses", passes on what it has learned from its neighbours, and picks routes based on its own business preferences. Out of all these local decisions, global routes emerge.</p>
<p>BGP is powerful and, by design, built on trust. That occasionally shows:</p>
<ul>
<li>In 2008, a misconfigured announcement from one provider made YouTube unreachable for large parts of the world for about two hours.</li>
<li>In 2021, Facebook withdrew its own BGP announcements during maintenance and disappeared from the internet for several hours, together with its own internal tools.</li>
</ul>
<p>Security extensions such as <em>RPKI</em>, which lets networks verify who may announce which addresses, are increasingly used to prevent such accidents and deliberate hijacks.</p>
<h2 id="why-this-matters-at-home-and-in-the-office">Why This Matters at Home and in the Office</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Latency has a floor.</strong> For services far away, physics sets a minimum delay that no tariff can buy away. At home, <a href="/src/blog/video-calls-bufferbloat/">bufferbloat</a> usually adds far more delay than distance does.</li>
<li><strong>Your provider's connections matter.</strong> How well a provider is connected through peering and transit affects speeds to certain services, especially in the evening. It is one of the hidden details when <a href="/src/blog/fiber-cable-dsl-5g/">choosing a connection</a>.</li>
<li><strong>IPv6 is part of the future backbone.</strong> More and more of this traffic travels over <a href="/src/blog/ipv6-at-home-and-in-the-office/">IPv6</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>The internet's backbone is a federation of independent networks that agree to carry each other's traffic: through exchange points like the VIX, across undersea cables and guided by BGP, a protocol that runs on cooperation. Thanks to content delivery networks, most of your clicks end surprisingly close to home.
