Passwords, Passkeys and Two‑Factor: The Foundation of Everything Else

Every router, NAS and email account is only as safe as its login. Here is a setup that is secure and still easy to live with.

Firewalls, VPNs and segmented networks all assume one thing: that the person logging in is really you. In practice, most account takeovers do not involve clever hacking. They use passwords that were reused somewhere else, leaked in a breach, guessed, or typed into a convincing fake login page.

The good news: three habits close almost all of these doors, and none of them requires remembering more.

1. A Password Manager

The single most effective step is a password manager. It creates a long, random, different password for every account, remembers them for you and fills them in. You only remember one strong master password.

  • Options: the managers built into Apple and Google devices, or dedicated apps such as Bitwarden, 1Password or KeePassXC.
  • Start with what matters most: your email account (it can reset every other password), banking, the password manager itself, then everything else as you log in.
  • Bonus: a password manager only fills in passwords on the real website. On a fake page with a look‑alike address, it stays silent, which is a useful warning sign. If you did type a password into a fake page, the first 60 minutes matter most.

What a Good Password Looks Like

The password manager generates most of your passwords, but a few you still have to come up with yourself: the master password, your computer login, perhaps the Wi‑Fi. For those, a few rules matter more than everything you were told about special characters:

  • Length beats complexity. Every extra character multiplies the work for an attacker. The US standards body NIST now recommends at least 15 characters for a password that protects an account on its own, and no forced mix of symbols.
  • Random, not clever. Summer2026! or P@ssw0rd look complex but are among the first things attackers try. Names, birthdays, pet names, football clubs and keyboard rows (qwertz, 123456) are just as easy to guess.
  • A passphrase is easiest to remember. Four or five random, unrelated words, such as lantern cobalt meadow ferry, are long, hard to guess and easy to type. "Random" means picked by chance, not a quote or a song line. And don't use this example.
  • One password per account. Even a perfect password is worthless once it leaks from one website and gets tried on all the others.
  • Change it when there's a reason, not on a schedule. Forced changes every 90 days just lead to Summer2026! turning into Autumn2026!. Change a password when it has leaked or you have typed it somewhere suspicious.

Rule of thumb: If you can remember it easily and nobody who knows you could guess it, it's a good password. If it has to be complicated to be safe, it's too short.

2. Two‑Factor Authentication

Two‑factor authentication (2FA) adds a second proof to the password: something you have, like your phone or a security key. A stolen password alone is then not enough. Not all methods are equally strong:

Method How it works Verdict
SMS code A code sent by text message Better than nothing; vulnerable to SIM swapping and phishing
Authenticator app A six‑digit code that changes every 30 seconds Good; can still be phished on a fake page
Push approval Tap "yes" in an app Convenient; beware of approving requests you did not start
Security key or passkey Cryptographic login bound to the real website Best; cannot be phished

Turn on the best method each service offers, starting with email, banking, cloud storage and anything with admin rights.

Worth knowing: Save the recovery codes each service offers when you enable 2FA, in your password manager or printed in a drawer. Without them, a lost phone can lock you out for good.

3. Passkeys: Logging In Without a Password

Passkeys are the successor to passwords, supported by Apple, Google, Microsoft and a growing number of websites. Instead of a password, your device holds a cryptographic key and you confirm the login with your fingerprint, face or device PIN.

  • Nothing to steal from the website: the site only stores a public key, which is useless to attackers.
  • Phishing‑resistant: a passkey only works on the exact website it was created for. A fake page simply gets nothing.
  • Synced across devices through your password manager or your Apple or Google account, so a new phone is not a problem.

Whenever a website offers "create a passkey", say yes.

Check Whether You Have Been Affected

The free service Have I Been Pwned shows whether your email address appeared in a known data breach. Many password managers check this automatically and warn about reused or leaked passwords. Treat every warning as a to‑do: change that password, and turn on 2FA if you have not already.

For Small Businesses

  • Require 2FA for every account, without exceptions for the boss or the shared mailbox. Email and administrator accounts first.
  • Use a business password manager with shared vaults, so that passwords for the router, the NAS or the website are not stored in a spreadsheet.
  • Prefer single sign‑on where possible: one well‑protected company account that logs in to many services means one place to enforce 2FA and one place to switch off when someone leaves. It also makes people joining and leaving much easier to handle.

Conclusion

Most break‑ins start with a login. A password manager makes every password unique, two‑factor authentication makes a stolen password worthless, and passkeys make phishing pointless. Set up the manager today, protect your email account with the strongest second factor it offers, and say yes the next time a website suggests a passkey. Everything else in your network security builds on this.
