15:T144c,<p>Firewalls, VPNs and <a href="/src/blog/segmenting-your-home-network/">segmented networks</a> all assume one thing: that the person logging in is really you. In practice, most account takeovers do not involve clever hacking. They use passwords that were reused somewhere else, leaked in a breach, guessed, or typed into a convincing fake login page.</p>
<p>The good news: three habits close almost all of these doors, and none of them requires remembering more.</p>
<h2 id="1-a-password-manager">1. A Password Manager</h2>
<p>The single most effective step is a password manager. It creates a long, random, <strong>different</strong> password for every account, remembers them for you and fills them in. You only remember one strong master password.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Options:</strong> the managers built into Apple and Google devices, or dedicated apps such as Bitwarden, 1Password or KeePassXC.</li>
<li><strong>Start with what matters most:</strong> your email account (it can reset every other password), banking, the password manager itself, then everything else as you log in.</li>
<li><strong>Bonus:</strong> a password manager only fills in passwords on the real website. On a fake page with a look‑alike address, it stays silent, which is a useful warning sign. If you did type a password into a fake page, the <a href="/src/blog/phishing-first-60-minutes/">first 60 minutes</a> matter most.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="2-twofactor-authentication">2. Two‑Factor Authentication</h2>
<p>Two‑factor authentication (2FA) adds a second proof to the password: something you have, like your phone or a security key. A stolen password alone is then not enough. Not all methods are equally strong:</p>
<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Method</th>
<th>How it works</th>
<th>Verdict</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>SMS code</td>
<td>A code sent by text message</td>
<td>Better than nothing; vulnerable to SIM swapping and phishing</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Authenticator app</td>
<td>A six‑digit code that changes every 30 seconds</td>
<td>Good; can still be phished on a fake page</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Push approval</td>
<td>Tap "yes" in an app</td>
<td>Convenient; beware of approving requests you did not start</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Security key or passkey</td>
<td>Cryptographic login bound to the real website</td>
<td>Best; cannot be phished</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
<p>Turn on the best method each service offers, starting with email, banking, cloud storage and anything with admin rights.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Worth knowing:</strong> Save the <strong>recovery codes</strong> each service offers when you enable 2FA, in your password manager or printed in a drawer. Without them, a lost phone can lock you out for good.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="3-passkeys-logging-in-without-a-password">3. Passkeys: Logging In Without a Password</h2>
<p>Passkeys are the successor to passwords, supported by Apple, Google, Microsoft and a growing number of websites. Instead of a password, your device holds a cryptographic key and you confirm the login with your fingerprint, face or device PIN.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Nothing to steal from the website:</strong> the site only stores a public key, which is useless to attackers.</li>
<li><strong>Phishing‑resistant:</strong> a passkey only works on the exact website it was created for. A fake page simply gets nothing.</li>
<li><strong>Synced across devices</strong> through your password manager or your Apple or Google account, so a new phone is not a problem.</li>
</ul>
<p>Whenever a website offers "create a passkey", say yes.</p>
<h2 id="check-whether-you-have-been-affected">Check Whether You Have Been Affected</h2>
<p>The free service <em>Have I Been Pwned</em> shows whether your email address appeared in a known data breach. Many password managers check this automatically and warn about reused or leaked passwords. Treat every warning as a to‑do: change that password, and turn on 2FA if you have not already.</p>
<h2 id="for-small-businesses">For Small Businesses</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Require 2FA for every account</strong>, without exceptions for the boss or the shared mailbox. Email and administrator accounts first.</li>
<li><strong>Use a business password manager</strong> with shared vaults, so that passwords for the <a href="/src/blog/router-hardening/">router</a>, the NAS or the website are not stored in a spreadsheet.</li>
<li><strong>Prefer single sign‑on</strong> where possible: one well‑protected company account that logs in to many services means one place to enforce 2FA and one place to switch off when someone leaves. It also makes <a href="/src/blog/onboarding-offboarding/">people joining and leaving</a> much easier to handle.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Most break‑ins start with a login. A password manager makes every password unique, two‑factor authentication makes a stolen password worthless, and passkeys make phishing pointless. Set up the manager today, protect your email account with the strongest second factor it offers, and say yes the next time a website suggests a passkey.
