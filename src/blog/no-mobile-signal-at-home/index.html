No Mobile Signal at Home? Wi‑Fi Calling and What Else Helps

Why phones lose reception indoors, how Wi‑Fi calling works around it, and which fixes are legal in Austria and which are not.

The internet at home is fast, the Wi‑Fi reaches every room, and still the phone shows a single bar in the kitchen and nothing at all in the home office. Calls break up mid‑sentence, text messages arrive in bursts, and the only reliable spot is the balcony. Out on the street, the same phone has full reception.

The good news: in most homes you can fix this without a new contract or new hardware, because the connection you already have can carry your calls.

Why the Signal Stops at the Door

Mobile networks are planned from the outside. Masts cover streets and neighbourhoods, and the signal has to get through the building shell to reach you. Some materials make that hard:

  • Energy‑efficient windows. Modern glazing often has a thin metallic coating that keeps heat in, and it reflects radio waves just as well. Older buildings with new windows are a classic case.
  • Reinforced concrete and steel. Concrete ceilings, steel beams and lift shafts block a lot. Basements and inner rooms suffer most.
  • Foil‑backed insulation. Insulation boards with an aluminium layer, common in renovated roofs, act like a shield.
  • The frequency. Low bands such as 700 or 800 MHz pass through walls far better than the 3.5 GHz band much of 5G uses. That is why a phone can show 5G at the front door and almost nothing two rooms further in.

So the problem is rarely the phone and often not the network either. It is the last few centimetres of wall and glass.

Wi‑Fi Calling: Your Router Becomes the Mast

Wi‑Fi calling (also called WLAN Call or VoWiFi) lets your phone make normal calls and send text messages over any Wi‑Fi network with internet access. You keep your number and the usual phone app, and the person you are calling notices no difference. Within Austria, calls are billed as usual from your tariff.

Behind the scenes, the phone builds an encrypted tunnel through the internet to your mobile operator, which treats the call like one from a mast. It simply arrived by a different route.

How to Switch It On

  1. Check your tariff. A1, Magenta and Drei offer Wi‑Fi calling, but whether it is included depends on the tariff, and not every smaller provider supports it. The provider's help pages will tell you.
  2. Turn it on in the phone. On an iPhone, look under Settings → Phone → Wi‑Fi Calling. On Android, it is usually in the phone app's settings or the SIM settings. If the switch is missing, your phone or tariff does not support it with this provider.
  3. Test it. Switch on flight mode, turn Wi‑Fi back on and make a call. If it connects, Wi‑Fi calling works. Many phones show a small "Wi‑Fi" next to the operator name while it is active.

Quick win: Many people who struggle with reception at home already have a phone and tariff that support Wi‑Fi calling. It is just switched off.

What It Needs From Your Network

A call needs very little bandwidth, but like any voice call it is sensitive to delay:

  • Good Wi‑Fi where you actually take calls. If the home office is a Wi‑Fi dead zone too, the call has nowhere to go. Mesh Wi‑Fi explained shows when mesh helps and when a wired access point is better.
  • Low latency under load. A large upload in the background can make calls stutter. Smart queue management on the router fixes that, as described in why video calls stutter.
  • An open path to the operator. The tunnel uses IPsec over UDP ports 500 and 4500. Home routers allow this by default; some strict office, hotel or guest networks do not.

Limits Worth Knowing

  • Emergency calls. For 112, phones generally prefer the mobile network and can use any available network, even another operator's. Whether an emergency call also works over Wi‑Fi alone depends on operator and phone. If someone at home must be able to call for help from a room without reception, keep a landline or DECT phone within reach.
  • Power cuts. No electricity, no router, no Wi‑Fi calling. A small UPS for the router bridges short outages.
  • Walking out of the house. Many phones hand a running call over to the mobile network, but not every phone and network does it smoothly.
  • Abroad. Some tariffs bill Wi‑Fi calls from abroad differently or need an extra option. Check before you travel.

The Options Compared

If Wi‑Fi calling is not available, or the problem affects a whole building, there are other routes. They differ a lot in effort, and one of them is not allowed.

Wi‑Fi calling Calls run over your internet connection Free, five minutes Try this first
Better Wi‑Fi coverage Mesh or access points bring Wi‑Fi, and therefore calls, to every room Moderate Needed if Wi‑Fi has dead zones too

4G/5G router by the window or with an outdoor antenna Catches the signal where it is strong and shares it as Wi‑Fi Moderate Good for rural houses, together with Wi‑Fi calling
Landline number with a DECT phone Calls over the fixed line, independent of mobile reception Low, if your router has DECT Reliable, also in emergencies

Why Repeaters Are Off the Table

Online shops sell mobile repeaters and boosters that promise full bars indoors. In Austria, the Fernmeldebüro, the telecommunications authority, is clear: only operators of public mobile networks may run repeaters and femtocells (small private mobile cells) on mobile frequencies. Cheap boosters often disturb the network for everyone nearby, operators detect that, and illegally operating a radio device can be fined under the Telecommunications Act.

A 4G or 5G router with an external antenna is a different thing. It connects to the network like a phone and passes the connection on as Wi‑Fi, without re‑transmitting the mobile signal. Mobile internet as a connection type is covered in fibre, cable, DSL or 5G.

Rule of thumb: Receiving the mobile signal and passing it on as Wi‑Fi is fine. Sending it out again as a mobile signal is the operator's job.

Find Out Where It Is Bad

Before buying anything, walk around for ten minutes:

  1. Note the signal per room. Many phones show the exact strength in dBm in the status or SIM information. Roughly, around −80 dBm is strong, and below about −110 dBm calls start to struggle. That tells you more than the bars, which every manufacturer draws differently.
  2. Compare window and room. Good at an open window, weak behind closed glass: the windows are the culprit, and a router by the window will help.
  3. Compare networks. Coverage maps are drawn for outdoors. Ask the neighbours which network works for them, or test a cheap prepaid SIM. The regulator RTR's free Netztest app helps you compare measurements.

For Small Businesses

  • Make Wi‑Fi calling part of the setup for company phones, and check that the firewall and guest network let it through.
  • Plan Wi‑Fi with calls in mind, including meeting rooms, stairwells and the storage room where people take calls.
  • Ask your operator about indoor coverage if the building is large or many people are affected.
  • Consider IP telephony for desk phones, so being reachable does not depend on mobile reception at all. Telephony for small businesses explains what that involves.

Conclusion

Weak reception at home is usually caused by the building, not the network. Switch on Wi‑Fi calling, make sure your Wi‑Fi reaches the rooms where you take calls, and test it once in flight mode. If that is not enough, a 4G/5G router by the window, another network or a landline number with a DECT phone are the next steps, and boosters from online shops are not. Then the kitchen stops being the room where calls drop, and the balcony is just a balcony again.
