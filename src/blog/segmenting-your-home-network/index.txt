15:T1323,<p>A typical home network has grown over years: laptops and phones, a TV, a printer, a robot vacuum, smart plugs, a doorbell camera, the children's console (<a href="/src/blog/parental-controls-network/">parental controls</a> are a separate topic) and whatever the guests bring along. All of them usually sit in one flat network where every device can talk to every other one.</p>
<p>That is convenient — and it means the cheapest, least-updated device in the house has the same reach as your laptop.</p>
<h2 id="why-separation-matters">Why Separation Matters</h2>
<p>Smart devices are often built on a tight budget. Some receive security updates for only a few years, some never. If one of them is compromised, an attacker who is inside your network can try to reach your computers, your NAS with family photos or your router's admin page. How to keep them useful while separated is part of <a href="/src/blog/smart-home-matter-thread-zigbee/">the smart home article</a>.</p>
<p>Segmentation means dividing the network into zones that cannot reach each other freely. A compromised light bulb then stays a compromised light bulb.</p>
<h2 id="three-zones-for-most-homes">Three Zones for Most Homes</h2>
<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Zone</th>
<th>What goes in</th>
<th>Can reach</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Trusted</td>
<td>Laptops, phones, NAS, work devices</td>
<td>Internet and the other zones where needed</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Smart home / IoT</td>
<td>TV, speakers, cameras, plugs, vacuum</td>
<td>Internet only (plus what you explicitly allow)</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Guests</td>
<td>Visitors' phones and laptops</td>
<td>Internet only</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
<p>Even this simple split removes most of the risk.</p>
<h2 id="how-to-do-it">How to Do It</h2>
<h3 id="level-1-the-guest-network">Level 1: The Guest Network</h3>
<p>Nearly every current router offers a guest Wi‑Fi. Devices in it can reach the internet but not your main network. It is the easiest place to start — and it works not only for visitors but also for smart devices.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Quick win:</strong> Create a guest network, give it its own password and move all smart-home gadgets that only need the internet into it.</p>
</blockquote>
<h3 id="level-2-a-dedicated-iot-network">Level 2: A Dedicated IoT Network</h3>
<p>More capable routers and mesh systems let you create several separate networks, often called <a href="/src/blog/vlans-managed-switches/">VLANs (virtual LANs)</a>. You can then run a dedicated IoT network next to the guest network, each with its own rules.</p>
<p>This is useful when you want guests and smart devices separated from each other as well, or when you want to allow specific exceptions.</p>
<h3 id="level-3-firewall-rules-between-zones">Level 3: Firewall Rules Between Zones</h3>
<p>With a firewall that understands multiple networks, you can set precise rules — for example: "the trusted network may reach the IoT network, but not the other way round". This lets your phone control the lights while the lights cannot start conversations with your laptop.</p>
<h2 id="the-annoying-part-casting-and-discovery">The Annoying Part: Casting and Discovery</h2>
<p>Separation has one well-known side effect. Features such as screen casting, AirPlay or printer discovery rely on devices finding each other automatically in the same network (via mDNS/Bonjour). Put the TV in another zone and the phone may no longer see it.</p>
<p>There are three ways around this:</p>
<ol>
<li>Keep devices you cast to — TV, speakers — in the trusted network and isolate only the rest.</li>
<li>Use a router that supports an <strong>mDNS reflector</strong> or repeater, which passes discovery messages between zones.</li>
<li>Accept a little inconvenience for the devices you rarely use.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="other-habits-that-help">Other Habits That Help</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Change default passwords</strong> on every device with a login, especially cameras and the router itself.</li>
<li><strong>Turn off UPnP</strong> on the router if you do not need it, so devices cannot open ports to the internet on their own.</li>
<li><strong>Update firmware</strong> regularly and retire devices that no longer receive updates. The <a href="/src/blog/router-hardening/">router checklist</a> covers the device at the centre of it all.</li>
<li><strong>Keep an inventory.</strong> A simple list of what is connected — and why — makes it much easier to spot something that does not belong.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>You do not need enterprise equipment to make a home network much safer. A guest network for visitors and gadgets is a five-minute change that already isolates the riskiest devices.
