15:T2232,<p>The website was built ten years ago by a friend of the founder. He also "took care of the domain". Since then, the business has moved offices twice, changed its bank and switched email provider. One Monday, emails stop arriving and the website shows a parking page full of ads. The domain expired, the renewal notice went to an address nobody reads, and the friend is now living abroad.</p>
<p>The good news: taking real control of your domain takes an afternoon, and once it is done, it mostly runs by itself.</p>
<h2 id="why-the-domain-matters-so-much">Why the Domain Matters So Much</h2>
<p>A domain such as <code>example.at</code> looks like a detail of the website. In practice, it is the address book entry for almost everything a business does online:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Email:</strong> every address ending in <code>@example.at</code> stops working when the domain stops working.</li>
<li><strong>Website and online shop:</strong> customers and search engines find you by name.</li>
<li><strong>Logins:</strong> password resets for many services go to email addresses on your domain. Whoever controls the domain can reset those passwords.</li>
<li><strong>Trust:</strong> your <a href="/src/blog/email-spf-dkim-dmarc/">email authentication records</a> and certificates are tied to it.</li>
</ul>
<p>That last point makes the domain a security topic, not only an administrative one. If someone takes it over, they can receive your email.</p>
<h2 id="four-roles-you-should-be-able-to-name">Four Roles You Should Be Able to Name</h2>
<p>Behind every domain are several parties, and they are often confused. Knowing who is who is the first step to control.</p>
<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Role</th>
<th>What it does</th>
<th>Who it should be</th>
<th>Verdict</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Registrant (holder)</td>
<td>The legal owner of the domain</td>
<td>The business itself, not an employee or agency</td>
<td>The one that matters most</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Registrar</td>
<td>The company you rent the domain from and pay yearly</td>
<td>A provider with an account in the business's name</td>
<td>Must be accessible to at least two people</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>DNS hosting</td>
<td>Answers where web and email live</td>
<td>Often the registrar, sometimes a separate service</td>
<td>Fine to separate, but document it</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Web and email hosting</td>
<td>Where the website and mailboxes actually run</td>
<td>Whoever suits you</td>
<td>Can change without touching ownership</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
<p>For Austrian <code>.at</code> domains, the registry is nic.at. You usually do not deal with it directly but through a registrar, and your registrar's account shows who is entered as holder.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Worth knowing:</strong> A web agency or IT partner can manage everything for you. That is fine, as long as the <strong>holder</strong> is your business and you have your own access to the registrar account. Management can be handed over; ownership is much harder to get back.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="how-to-check-your-domain-today">How to Check Your Domain Today</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Find the registrar.</strong> Look at the last domain invoice, or search for your domain in a public domain lookup (<em>whois</em>). For data protection reasons, it often hides the holder, but it usually names the registrar.</li>
<li><strong>Log in to the registrar account.</strong> If nobody can, that is the most important finding of the afternoon.</li>
<li><strong>Check the holder</strong> in the registrar account or ask the registrar. Is it the business with its current legal name? Or the founder privately, a former employee, the agency?</li>
<li><strong>Check the contact email.</strong> Renewal notices and transfer confirmations go there. It should be a shared address such as <code>domains@example.at</code> that more than one person reads, ideally not on the same domain, so you still receive warnings when the domain itself has a problem.</li>
<li><strong>Check the payment method and expiry date.</strong> An expired company card is the most common reason domains lapse.</li>
<li><strong>Write it down.</strong> Registrar, account, holder, expiry date and where DNS is hosted, stored in the company's password manager.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="keeping-it-from-expiring">Keeping It From Expiring</h2>
<p>Domains are rented for a year or more at a time. When the period runs out without payment, the domain stops working, and after a grace period that depends on the domain ending, it is released. Getting it back during the grace period usually costs a restoration fee. After that, anyone can register it, and expired business domains are popular with speculators and scammers because email still arrives for them.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Turn on auto‑renewal</strong> at the registrar.</li>
<li><strong>Use a payment method that does not expire quietly,</strong> or put the card's expiry date in the calendar.</li>
<li><strong>Consider renewing for several years</strong> for your main domain, if the registrar offers it.</li>
<li><strong>Put the expiry date in a shared calendar</strong> anyway, a month in advance. Auto‑renewal fails more often than people think.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="locked-doors-at-the-registrar">Locked Doors at the Registrar</h2>
<p>The registrar account is as valuable as the domain itself. Protect it like your bank account:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Two‑factor authentication</strong> for every person with access, ideally with an app or security key. The reasons are explained in <a href="/src/blog/passwords-passkeys-2fa/">passwords, passkeys and 2FA</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Transfer lock</strong> switched on, if the registrar offers it. A domain then cannot move to another registrar without first unlocking it in your account.</li>
<li><strong>The transfer code</strong> (auth code) is the key for moving a domain. Never send it by email to someone who asks for it unexpectedly.</li>
<li><strong>DNSSEC</strong> if your DNS host supports it. It protects the answers about your domain from being forged on the way, as described in <a href="/src/blog/dns-explained/">DNS explained</a>.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="the-letters-and-emails-that-are-not-invoices">The Letters and Emails That Are Not Invoices</h2>
<p>Every domain owner eventually receives messages that look like bills: "Your domain expires", "Register your name in Asia before someone else does", or an urgent email saying the domain has been suspended and needs "re‑activation" with one click. Many of them are offers from unrelated companies dressed up as invoices; others are plain phishing pages that collect card details. Watchlist Internet regularly warns about such messages in Austria.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> Only one company sends you real invoices for a domain: your registrar. If the sender is not the one in your documentation, do not pay and do not click. When in doubt, log in to the registrar directly, not through the link.</p>
</blockquote>
<p>If someone already clicked and entered details, <a href="/src/blog/phishing-first-60-minutes/">the first 60 minutes after a phishing click</a> are what counts.</p>
<h2 id="when-people-leave-and-partners-change">When People Leave and Partners Change</h2>
<p>Domains are often lost not through attacks, but through ordinary changes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>An employee leaves</strong> whose private email was the registrar contact. Add the registrar account to your <a href="/src/blog/onboarding-offboarding/">offboarding checklist</a> and change the contact before the last day.</li>
<li><strong>The agency relationship ends.</strong> Ask for the domain to be moved to an account in your name, or for the holder to be changed, while you are still on good terms.</li>
<li><strong>The business changes its name or legal form.</strong> Update the holder data; a domain registered to a company that no longer exists invites trouble.</li>
<li><strong>Extra domains</strong> (old names, typo variants) need the same care. Even if they never send email, attackers like unwatched domains. Keep them in the same account and on the same list.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Your domain carries your email, your website and the keys to many logins, so it deserves more attention than a yearly invoice. Find out who the registrar is, make sure the business is the holder, and get your own login with two‑factor authentication. Turn on auto‑renewal, send notices to a shared address that does not depend on the domain itself, and keep everything in the password manager. Ignore any invoice that does not come from your registrar.
