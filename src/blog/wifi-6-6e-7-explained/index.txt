15:T164b,<p>The box says <em>BE19000</em>, <em>tri‑band</em> and <em>up to 19 Gbps</em>. Your laptop then reports a few hundred megabits, in the best case. Wi‑Fi marketing adds up the theoretical maximum of every band and every antenna, numbers no single device will ever see.</p>
<p>The good news: the generations themselves are easy to tell apart, and each one solves a specific problem. Knowing which problem you have tells you whether an upgrade helps.</p>

<h2 id="wifi-6-built-for-busy-networks">Wi‑Fi 6: Built for Busy Networks</h2>
<p>Wi‑Fi 6 is less about peak speed than about <strong>many devices at the same time</strong>. The key technology, <em>OFDMA</em>, lets the router serve several devices in one transmission instead of one after another, like a delivery van dropping parcels on one round instead of driving back and forth. <em>Target Wake Time</em> lets battery devices agree on when to wake up, which saves power in phones and sensors.</p>
<p>If your home has dozens of devices, smart home gadgets, phones, TVs and laptops, Wi‑Fi 6 makes the whole network calmer, even if each device is not much faster.</p>
<h2 id="wifi-6e-a-new-motorway">Wi‑Fi 6E: A New Motorway</h2>
<p>Wi‑Fi 6E is Wi‑Fi 6 plus the <strong>6 GHz band</strong>. That band is new and empty: no old devices, no neighbours' networks from ten years ago, no baby monitors. In Europe the lower part of the band, 480 MHz wide, is available. That is enough for three 160 MHz channels or six 80 MHz channels, more clean room than the entire crowded 2.4 GHz band.</p>
<p>The trade‑off is range. 6 GHz signals fade faster through walls than 5 GHz, so 6E shines in the same room or with an access point per floor, which is why <a href="/src/blog/home-network-cabling/">cabled access points</a> and 6E go well together. On 6 GHz, WPA3 is mandatory, which also means old security modes are simply not possible.</p>
<h2 id="wifi-7-wider-and-smarter">Wi‑Fi 7: Wider and Smarter</h2>
<p>Wi‑Fi 7 builds on 6E with three main changes:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>320 MHz channels</strong> on 6 GHz, twice as wide as before. In Europe there is room for one of them, so they help most in quiet surroundings.</li>
<li><strong>4096‑QAM</strong>, a denser way of encoding data. It adds around 20 percent speed, but only at very close range with an excellent signal.</li>
<li><strong>Multi‑Link Operation (MLO)</strong>: a device can use several bands at once, for example 5 and 6 GHz, or switch between them without dropping the connection. This is the feature you are most likely to notice, as more stable connections and lower latency.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="why-your-laptop-never-sees-the-number-on-the-box">Why Your Laptop Never Sees the Number on the Box</h2>
<p>The headline figure is the sum of all bands, at maximum channel width, with four or more antenna streams. A typical phone or laptop has <strong>two</strong> antenna streams and uses <strong>one</strong> band at a time. Walls, distance and neighbours reduce the rate further. As a rough guide, a good Wi‑Fi 6 connection in the same room delivers several hundred megabits per second, Wi‑Fi 6E and 7 can reach well over a gigabit under ideal conditions.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> Compare routers by features (6 GHz, MLO, number of Ethernet ports, update policy), not by the big number on the box.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="when-an-upgrade-is-worth-it">When an Upgrade Is Worth It</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Many devices, older router (Wi‑Fi 5 or earlier):</strong> a Wi‑Fi 6 router or better makes a real difference.</li>
<li><strong>Apartment building with dozens of networks:</strong> 6E or 7 gives your newer devices a quiet band to escape to.</li>
<li><strong>Fast fibre connection over 1 Gbit/s:</strong> Wi‑Fi 7 and multi‑gigabit Ethernet ports let you actually use it.</li>
<li><strong>Coverage problems:</strong> no new standard fixes walls. More access points, ideally cabled, do. See <a href="/src/blog/why-your-wifi-is-slow/">why your Wi‑Fi is slow</a>. A <a href="/src/blog/mesh-wifi-explained/">mesh system</a> with a good backhaul is the easiest way to add them.</li>
</ul>
<p>Remember that both sides must support a standard. A Wi‑Fi 7 router does nothing for a five‑year‑old laptop, which keeps talking Wi‑Fi 5. The good news: new routers stay compatible with all older devices.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Wi‑Fi 6 makes busy networks efficient, 6E adds a clean new band, and Wi‑Fi 7 makes connections wider and more stable through multi‑link operation. The big numbers on the box are theoretical sums you will not see.
