Wi‑Fi 6, 6E and 7: What the Numbers Really Mean

Router boxes promise tens of gigabits. Here is what each generation actually changes, and when an upgrade is worth it.

The box says BE19000, tri‑band and up to 19 Gbps. Your laptop then reports a few hundred megabits, in the best case. Wi‑Fi marketing adds up the theoretical maximum of every band and every antenna, numbers no single device will ever see.

The good news: the generations themselves are easy to tell apart, and each one solves a specific problem. Knowing which problem you have tells you whether an upgrade helps.

The Generations in Plain Words

Since 2018, Wi‑Fi standards have simple numbers instead of the old 802.11 letters:

Name Standard Bands What it brought
Wi‑Fi 4 802.11n 2.4 and 5 GHz Multiple antennas, the first "fast" Wi‑Fi
Wi‑Fi 5 802.11ac 5 GHz Much faster on 5 GHz
Wi‑Fi 6 802.11ax 2.4 and 5 GHz Efficiency with many devices at once
Wi‑Fi 6E 802.11ax adds 6 GHz A new, uncrowded band
Wi‑Fi 7 802.11be 2.4, 5 and 6 GHz Wider channels, multi‑link operation

Wi‑Fi 6: Built for Busy Networks

Wi‑Fi 6 is less about peak speed than about many devices at the same time. The key technology, OFDMA, lets the router serve several devices in one transmission instead of one after another, like a delivery van dropping parcels on one round instead of driving back and forth. Target Wake Time lets battery devices agree on when to wake up, which saves power in phones and sensors.

If your home has dozens of devices, smart home gadgets, phones, TVs and laptops, Wi‑Fi 6 makes the whole network calmer, even if each device is not much faster.

Wi‑Fi 6E: A New Motorway

Wi‑Fi 6E is Wi‑Fi 6 plus the 6 GHz band. That band is new and empty: no old devices, no neighbours' networks from ten years ago, no baby monitors. In Europe the lower part of the band, 480 MHz wide, is available. That is enough for three 160 MHz channels or six 80 MHz channels, more clean room than the entire crowded 2.4 GHz band.

The trade‑off is range. 6 GHz signals fade faster through walls than 5 GHz, so 6E shines in the same room or with an access point per floor, which is why cabled access points and 6E go well together. On 6 GHz, WPA3 is mandatory, which also means old security modes are simply not possible.

Wi‑Fi 7: Wider and Smarter

Wi‑Fi 7 builds on 6E with three main changes:

  • 320 MHz channels on 6 GHz, twice as wide as before. In Europe there is room for one of them, so they help most in quiet surroundings.
  • 4096‑QAM, a denser way of encoding data. It adds around 20 percent speed, but only at very close range with an excellent signal.
  • Multi‑Link Operation (MLO): a device can use several bands at once, for example 5 and 6 GHz, or switch between them without dropping the connection. This is the feature you are most likely to notice, as more stable connections and lower latency.

Why Your Laptop Never Sees the Number on the Box

The headline figure is the sum of all bands, at maximum channel width, with four or more antenna streams. A typical phone or laptop has two antenna streams and uses one band at a time. Walls, distance and neighbours reduce the rate further. As a rough guide, a good Wi‑Fi 6 connection in the same room delivers several hundred megabits per second, Wi‑Fi 6E and 7 can reach well over a gigabit under ideal conditions.

Rule of thumb: Compare routers by features (6 GHz, MLO, number of Ethernet ports, update policy), not by the big number on the box.

When an Upgrade Is Worth It

  • Many devices, older router (Wi‑Fi 5 or earlier): a Wi‑Fi 6 router or better makes a real difference.
  • Apartment building with dozens of networks: 6E or 7 gives your newer devices a quiet band to escape to.
  • Fast fibre connection over 1 Gbit/s: Wi‑Fi 7 and multi‑gigabit Ethernet ports let you actually use it.
  • Coverage problems: no new standard fixes walls. More access points, ideally cabled, do. See why your Wi‑Fi is slow. A mesh system with a good backhaul is the easiest way to add them.

Remember that both sides must support a standard. A Wi‑Fi 7 router does nothing for a five‑year‑old laptop, which keeps talking Wi‑Fi 5. The good news: new routers stay compatible with all older devices.

Conclusion

Wi‑Fi 6 makes busy networks efficient, 6E adds a clean new band, and Wi‑Fi 7 makes connections wider and more stable through multi‑link operation. The big numbers on the box are theoretical sums you will not see. Upgrade when you have many devices, a crowded neighbourhood or a connection faster than your Wi‑Fi, and fix coverage with placement and cables rather than with a new generation.
