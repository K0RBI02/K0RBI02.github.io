Why Video Calls Stutter When Someone Uploads a File

Your speed test looks great, yet calls freeze whenever the network is busy. The culprit is called bufferbloat, and it can be fixed.

The connection tests at 300 Mbit/s, and still the video call turns into a slideshow every evening. Faces freeze, voices sound robotic, and it always seems to happen when someone else uploads holiday photos or the laptop starts its cloud backup. The bandwidth is clearly there. Something else is getting in the way.

The good news: this problem has a name, a simple test and, on many routers, a setting that fixes it.

Bandwidth Is Not the Same as Latency

A video call needs surprisingly little bandwidth, a few megabits per second. What it needs is low and stable latency: every packet should arrive within a few dozen milliseconds, and the delay should not jump around (that jumping is called jitter).

Downloads and uploads are the opposite. They want to push as much data as possible and do not care whether a packet waits a little longer. When both share one line, the bulk traffic usually wins.

What Bufferbloat Is

Routers and modems hold packets in a queue, a buffer, when the line is busy. A small buffer smooths out short bursts. A large buffer that fills completely becomes a waiting room: your call's packets queue behind thousands of photo packets, and latency rises from 20 milliseconds to 300, 500 or more.

That is bufferbloat. It hits the upload direction hardest, because on many cable and DSL connections the upload is much slower than the download and fills up quickly.

How to Test It

Ordinary speed tests report the latency of an idle line. You need the latency under load:

  1. Run a speed test that shows loaded latency, for example Cloudflare's speed test or the Waveform bufferbloat test.
  2. Compare the latency at rest with the latency during download and upload.
Increase under load Meaning
Less than 30 ms Excellent, calls stay smooth
30–100 ms Noticeable during heavy use
More than 100 ms Bufferbloat: calls and games will suffer

The Fix: Smart Queue Management

Modern routers can manage their queues intelligently. The technique is usually called SQM (smart queue management), sometimes Smart Queues, adaptive QoS or similar. Algorithms like fq_codel and CAKE keep queues short and give small, time‑critical packets a fast lane past the bulk traffic.

  • Set the limits slightly below your real speed, around 90–95 percent of what you measured. The router can only manage the queue if it is the bottleneck, not the modem behind it.
  • Measure again. Latency under load should now barely rise. You give up a few percent of peak speed in exchange for calls that stay smooth.
  • If your router has no such feature, the prioritisation settings for devices or applications are a second‑best option: give the work laptop or the phone used for calls priority.

Worth knowing: Many provider routers lack SQM. Some let you put them in bridge mode so your own router does the job, see the router you get.

Wi‑Fi Can Add Its Own Delay

Even with a perfect internet line, Wi‑Fi adds latency when many devices compete for airtime or the signal is weak. For calls that matter:

  • Use a cable for the desk you work at, or at least a strong 5 GHz or 6 GHz connection. If cables are out of the question, a mesh system with a good backhaul is the next best thing.
  • Move away from crowded 2.4 GHz. It is the band most likely to stall under load.
  • Keep large transfers off the call device. Cloud sync on the same laptop competes directly with the call.

See why your Wi‑Fi is slow for the details.

For Small Offices

Several people on calls while backups run to the cloud is the textbook bufferbloat situation. Two habits help: The same applies to phones over the internet.

  • Enable SQM on the office router, or choose one that has it.
  • Schedule big uploads, such as off‑site backups, for the night, or limit their bandwidth during working hours.

Conclusion

When calls stutter despite a fast connection, look at latency under load, not at the speed test headline. Bufferbloat is common, easy to measure and often fixed by switching on smart queue management with limits just below your real speed. Add a cable for the desk, keep big uploads out of working hours, and the next video call stays smooth even while the holiday photos upload.
