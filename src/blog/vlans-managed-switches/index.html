VLANs and Managed Switches: Separate Networks on One Set of Cables

Tagged, untagged, trunk, PVID. What the switch settings mean, and a simple plan for separating guests, smart devices and work.

Separating your network into zones is one of the most effective security steps, as described in segmenting your home network. With a single router and a guest Wi‑Fi you get surprisingly far. Beyond that, the next step is VLANs, and their settings look intimidating at first: tagged, untagged, trunk, PVID, native VLAN.

The good news: it all follows from one simple idea, and a small plan on paper prevents nearly every mistake.

The Idea: Labels on Packets

A VLAN (virtual LAN) is a separate network that shares cables and switches with others. Packets are kept apart by a small label, the VLAN ID, a number between 1 and 4094 defined in the standard 802.1Q. A switch that understands these labels, a managed switch, only delivers a packet to ports that belong to the same VLAN.

Devices in different VLANs cannot talk to each other directly. Traffic between them has to pass through the router or firewall, which decides with rules what is allowed.

Tagged and Untagged Ports

Each switch port can do one of two jobs, and this is the part that confuses everyone at first:

  • Untagged (access) port: connects a normal device, such as a PC or a printer, that knows nothing about VLANs. The switch adds the label when packets come in and removes it when they go out. The port belongs to exactly one VLAN, set by its PVID (port VLAN ID).
  • Tagged (trunk) port: connects devices that understand labels: the router, another switch, an access point. Packets keep their labels, so many VLANs can travel over one cable.

Rule of thumb: Devices get untagged ports. Network equipment, routers, switches and access points, gets tagged ports.

A Plan for a Home or Small Office

Write it down before touching any setting:

VLAN ID Name Network Who is in it
10 Main 192.168.10.0/24 Your computers and phones
20 Smart home 192.168.20.0/24 TVs, plugs, cameras, speakers
30 Guests 192.168.30.0/24 Visitors, internet only
99 Management 192.168.99.0/24 Admin pages of switches, access points, router

Using the VLAN ID in the address (10 → 192.168.10.x) makes it easy to see at a glance where a device belongs. The router gets an interface and a DHCP server for each VLAN.

Wi‑Fi Joins In

Access points that support VLANs map each Wi‑Fi network name to a VLAN: the main network to 10, the smart home network to 20, guests to 30. The access point is connected with a tagged port, so all three travel over its single cable.

Rules Between the Zones

With the VLANs in place, the firewall rules decide what crosses over. A sensible start:

  • Main may reach everything.
  • Smart home may reach the internet, but not the main network, except for answers to connections the main network started.
  • Guests may reach the internet only.
  • Management is only reachable from the main network.

Pitfalls

  • Locking yourself out. Change the management VLAN last, and keep one port untagged in your current network while you test.
  • Device discovery stops. Casting to the TV and finding printers use multicast (mDNS), which does not cross VLANs. Enable an mDNS reflector on the router for the pairs that need it (printers, smart home).
  • VLAN 1 by default. Most switches put everything in VLAN 1 out of the box. Do not use it for anything important; leave unused ports in an unused VLAN or switch them off.
  • Mixing vendors. Switches and access points from different manufacturers name the same settings differently. Your plan on paper is the reference.

Conclusion

VLANs are separate networks sharing the same cables, kept apart by labels. Devices plug into untagged ports, network equipment talks over tagged ones, and the router decides with rules what may pass between zones. Draw the plan first, keep a way back while you configure, and take care of device discovery. Then one set of cables carries a network for work, one for gadgets and one for guests, each with its own front door.
