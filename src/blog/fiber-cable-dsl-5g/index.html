Fiber, Cable, DSL or 5G: What Your Connection Really Delivers

The advertised speed is only half the story. Latency, upload and stability matter just as much.

"Up to 500 Mbit/s" says very little about how a connection feels in daily use. Two lines with the same headline number can behave completely differently on a video call, during an evening of streaming or when uploading a large backup. To choose well — at home or for a business — you need to understand what sits between your router and the internet.

The Four Numbers That Matter

  • Download: How fast data reaches you. Important for streaming and downloads, but rarely the bottleneck above about 100 Mbit/s for a household.
  • Upload: How fast you send data. Video calls, cloud backups, sending large files and hosting anything depend on it. Often the overlooked weak spot.
  • Latency: The delay of each round trip, measured in milliseconds. It decides how responsive calls, remote desktops and games feel. Part of it is pure physics, as the journey of a single click shows.
  • Stability: Whether those values hold at 8 pm on a Sunday as well as at 10 am on a Tuesday. How to check that your line keeps its promise is explained in speed tests done right.

Fiber (FTTH and FTTB)

With fiber to the home (FTTH), an optical fiber runs all the way into your home and ends at a small box, the optical network terminal. It is the most capable access technology available: high, often symmetrical speeds, low latency and very little sensitivity to distance or weather.

With fiber to the building (FTTB), fiber reaches the basement and the last metres to each flat run over existing copper or coax wiring. Performance is usually still very good, but depends on that in-house wiring.

Worth knowing: "Fiber" in an advert does not always mean fiber to your home. Many DSL and cable products use fiber up to the street cabinet. Ask specifically whether the fiber ends in your home.

Cable (DOCSIS)

Cable internet uses the coaxial network originally built for TV. Modern DOCSIS standards deliver high download speeds that can reach the gigabit range.

The catch: the capacity of a cable segment is shared among all households connected to it. If the operator has not split the segments enough, speeds drop in the evening when everyone is online. Upload is traditionally much lower than download, although newer standards improve this.

DSL (VDSL)

DSL runs over the copper telephone line. With VDSL, the copper section only covers the distance from the street cabinet to your home, and techniques such as vectoring push speeds to roughly 100–250 Mbit/s on short lines.

The key factor is distance: every additional hundred metres of copper costs speed. Your neighbour three houses closer to the cabinet can get a noticeably better result. On the plus side, DSL is not shared like cable, so it tends to be stable in the evening.

Mobile: 5G and LTE at Home

Fixed wireless access — a 5G or LTE router in your home instead of a cable — has become a serious option where fixed lines are slow or unavailable. Good reception can deliver impressive speeds.

But the connection shares a radio cell with everyone else nearby, so speeds vary with the time of day and with cell load. Latency is usually higher and less consistent than on fiber. Many tariffs also come with fair-use limits. And if it is the phone that struggles indoors rather than the internet, Wi‑Fi calling is often the simplest fix.

For businesses, mobile internet has a second and very valuable role: as a backup line that takes over automatically when the main connection fails.

Hidden Details That Matter

Shared IPv4 Addresses (CGNAT and DS‑Lite)

Many providers no longer give each customer a public IPv4 address. Several customers share one, using carrier-grade NAT. For browsing this makes no difference. But if you want to reach devices at home from outside — a camera, a NAS, a VPN server — incoming connections over IPv4 will not work.

The solutions: use IPv6, ask the provider for a public IPv4 address (sometimes free, sometimes paid), or use a service that connects outward and avoids incoming connections entirely. How to check your own line and choose between them is explained in Can't reach home from outside?

The Router You Get

Many providers ship a router with their tariff. That is fine for most households, but check whether you are allowed to use your own and whether the provided device supports what you need: current Wi‑Fi standards, a guest network, VPN or proper firewall settings.

Business Tariffs

Business products typically add a contractual service level (fault repair times), static IP addresses and priority support. For a company that cannot work without internet, the repair time guarantee is often worth more than extra bandwidth. Static addresses also make remote access much simpler.

How to Choose

  1. If FTTH is available, it is almost always the best long-term choice.
  2. Look at the upload if you work from home, use cloud backups or run a business.
  3. Ask neighbours about evening speeds — especially with cable.
  4. For a business, plan a second line of a different technology (for example fiber plus 5G) rather than two lines of the same kind.

Conclusion

The best connection is the one whose weaknesses do not matter for how you use it. Compare upload, latency and stability, not just the big download number — and remember that the fastest line is worthless if the Wi‑Fi inside the building cannot keep up. If that is the weak spot, start with why your Wi‑Fi is slow.
