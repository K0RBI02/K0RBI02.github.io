15:T1178,<p>Most outages in small networks are not sudden. The backup failed silently three weeks ago. The disk in the NAS reported errors for a month. The website certificate expired on a Sunday. Someone would have noticed, if anyone had been looking.</p>
<p>The good news: monitoring for a home lab or a small office does not need an operations team. A handful of checks and a sensible alert channel cover the essentials.</p>
<h2 id="what-to-watch">What to Watch</h2>
<p>Start with the things that hurt most when they fail:</p>

<h2 id="three-kinds-of-monitoring">Three Kinds of Monitoring</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Uptime checks</strong> ask regularly: is this website, port or device reachable? <em>Uptime Kuma</em> is a popular self‑hosted tool with a friendly web interface.</li>
<li><strong>Metrics</strong> record numbers over time: CPU, memory, bandwidth, temperatures. Tools such as <em>Netdata</em> or <em>Prometheus</em> with <em>Grafana</em> turn them into graphs, which show trends before they become problems.</li>
<li><strong>Heartbeats</strong> turn the logic around for scheduled jobs: the backup <em>reports in</em> after each successful run, and you get an alert when the report does not arrive. Services like <em>Healthchecks.io</em> exist for exactly this "dead man's switch".</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="watch-from-outside-too">Watch From Outside Too</h2>
<p>A monitoring system inside your network cannot tell you that your network is offline, because it is offline as well. Add at least one check from <strong>outside</strong>, a small hosted service or a cheap server elsewhere, that tests your website and your internet connection from the internet's point of view.</p>
<h2 id="alerts-that-people-actually-read">Alerts That People Actually Read</h2>
<p>The fastest way to ruin monitoring is too many alerts. After the tenth false alarm, nobody looks anymore.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Alert only on what needs action.</strong> A CPU spike for a minute is not an emergency; a full disk is.</li>
<li><strong>Use a channel people see:</strong> push notifications to the phone (for example with <em>ntfy</em>), a team chat, or email for less urgent warnings.</li>
<li><strong>Wait before alerting.</strong> A website that is unreachable for one check is often a network hiccup; three failed checks in a row are a problem.</li>
<li><strong>Write down what to do</strong> for each alert, even one sentence. At 7 am on a Saturday, nobody wants to work it out from scratch.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="start-in-an-afternoon">Start in an Afternoon</h2>
<ol>
<li>Install an uptime tool and add your website, internet connection, router and NAS.</li>
<li>Turn on email or push notifications in the NAS for disk and RAID warnings (<a href="/src/blog/raid-and-nas-explained/">RAID and NAS</a>).</li>
<li>Add a heartbeat to the backup job.</li>
<li>Add a certificate expiry check for every public name.</li>
<li>Add one external check.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Monitoring is about hearing the small warnings before they become outages: the internet line, websites, certificates, backups, disks and the UPS. Start with uptime checks, heartbeats for backups and one check from outside, send alerts to a channel people actually read, and keep them few.
