Know Before Your Customers Do: Monitoring for Small Networks

The internet line, the backup, the certificate, the disk. A few simple checks warn you before something small becomes an outage.

Most outages in small networks are not sudden. The backup failed silently three weeks ago. The disk in the NAS reported errors for a month. The website certificate expired on a Sunday. Someone would have noticed, if anyone had been looking.

The good news: monitoring for a home lab or a small office does not need an operations team. A handful of checks and a sensible alert channel cover the essentials.

What to Watch

Start with the things that hurt most when they fail:

Check Why Typical warning sign
Internet connection and latency Everything depends on it Drops, high latency in the evening
Websites and services Customers notice first Not reachable, slow responses
Certificates Expired certificates break websites instantly Fewer than 14 days left
Backups The failure you only notice when you need them No successful run for 24 hours
Disks (SMART, RAID status) Disks warn before they fail Reallocated sectors, degraded array
UPS A dead battery only shows during an outage Failed self‑test, short runtime
Free disk space Full disks stop databases and backups Less than 15 percent free

Three Kinds of Monitoring

  • Uptime checks ask regularly: is this website, port or device reachable? Uptime Kuma is a popular self‑hosted tool with a friendly web interface.
  • Metrics record numbers over time: CPU, memory, bandwidth, temperatures. Tools such as Netdata or Prometheus with Grafana turn them into graphs, which show trends before they become problems.
  • Heartbeats turn the logic around for scheduled jobs: the backup reports in after each successful run, and you get an alert when the report does not arrive. Services like Healthchecks.io exist for exactly this "dead man's switch".

Watch From Outside Too

A monitoring system inside your network cannot tell you that your network is offline, because it is offline as well. Add at least one check from outside, a small hosted service or a cheap server elsewhere, that tests your website and your internet connection from the internet's point of view.

Alerts That People Actually Read

The fastest way to ruin monitoring is too many alerts. After the tenth false alarm, nobody looks anymore.

  • Alert only on what needs action. A CPU spike for a minute is not an emergency; a full disk is.
  • Use a channel people see: push notifications to the phone (for example with ntfy), a team chat, or email for less urgent warnings.
  • Wait before alerting. A website that is unreachable for one check is often a network hiccup; three failed checks in a row are a problem.
  • Write down what to do for each alert, even one sentence. At 7 am on a Saturday, nobody wants to work it out from scratch.

Start in an Afternoon

  1. Install an uptime tool and add your website, internet connection, router and NAS.
  2. Turn on email or push notifications in the NAS for disk and RAID warnings (RAID and NAS).
  3. Add a heartbeat to the backup job.
  4. Add a certificate expiry check for every public name.
  5. Add one external check.

Conclusion

Monitoring is about hearing the small warnings before they become outages: the internet line, websites, certificates, backups, disks and the UPS. Start with uptime checks, heartbeats for backups and one check from outside, send alerts to a channel people actually read, and keep them few. Then the next problem reaches you as a notification, not as a customer complaint.
