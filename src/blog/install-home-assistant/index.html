Your Smart Home, One App: Installing Home Assistant

The lights come from one brand, the thermostat from another, the smart plugs were on offer at the supermarket. Each one has its own app, its own account and its own cloud, and switching the living room to "evening" means opening three of them. Then one manufacturer shuts down its servers, and a perfectly good device turns into a plastic brick on the wall.

The good news: one small computer at home can bring all of it together in a single app, keep working when the internet is down, and doesn't need anyone's cloud. That computer runs Home Assistant, and this guide walks you through installing it.

Why Home Assistant

Home Assistant is free, open‑source software that talks to thousands of devices from different brands and runs entirely in your home. It knows your lights, plugs, sensors and heating, shows them on one dashboard and lets them work together: the hallway light switches on when the front door opens after sunset, whichever brand made the light and the sensor. How the radio standards behind that fit together is covered in Matter, Thread and Zigbee explained.

It also becomes the base for the next Hands‑on guide on this blog: an ad filter for the whole home runs as an app on the Home Assistant you install today.

Which Installation Fits

Home Assistant comes in several forms. Two of them matter for a home today:

  • Home Assistant OS is a complete operating system that contains Home Assistant and keeps itself up to date. Only this version can install apps (until early 2026 they were called add‑ons), the extra programs such as AdGuard Home or Tailscale.
  • Home Assistant Container runs Home Assistant inside Docker on a computer you already manage. It works, but has no apps, and you look after everything around it yourself.

The older Core and Supervised methods and 32‑bit systems lost official support with release 2025.12. If an old guide recommends them, skip it.

Hardware What it is Verdict
Home Assistant Green A ready‑made box from the makers of Home Assistant, with the OS pre‑installed The easiest start: plug in and go
Raspberry Pi 4 or 5 A small single‑board computer with a microSD card Cheap if you have one; the card is the weak spot
Used mini PC An office mini PC with an SSD Fast and roomy, great value second‑hand
Virtual machine Home Assistant OS on a server or NAS you already run Good for the home server owner, not for a first project
Container Docker on an existing Linux machine Only if you know Docker and don't need apps

Rule of thumb: If you are unsure, take Home Assistant OS on its own small device. Something that runs your heating and your lights shouldn't share a computer that you also restart for other reasons.

Used by Korbi: this guide's setup
  • Hardware: a Raspberry Pi 4 with 4 GB of RAM, running Home Assistant OS
  • Size: more than 80 devices, and it still runs smoothly
  • Takeaway: you don't need to start big. A Raspberry Pi is plenty for a typical home.

What You Need

  • The device itself: a Home Assistant Green, a Raspberry Pi 4 or 5 with at least 2 GB of RAM, or a 64‑bit mini PC. Check the current price in the shop; it has changed several times.
  • For a Raspberry Pi: a microSD card with at least 32 GB, ideally with the A2 label, the official power supply (not a phone charger, not a computer's USB port) and a card reader for your laptop.
  • For a mini PC: a USB stick with at least 8 GB for the installation.
  • A network cable. The installation needs a wired connection to your router. Wi‑Fi can come later, but a cable stays the more reliable choice.
  • A laptop or desktop on the same network, to write the image and to open the setup page.

1Step 1: Prepare the Device

Pick the option that matches your hardware.

Option A: Home Assistant Green

There is nothing to install. The Green ships with Home Assistant OS on its internal memory. Unpack it, and go straight to Step 2.

Option B: Raspberry Pi

The Raspberry Pi Imager writes Home Assistant OS onto the card for you:

  1. Install Raspberry Pi Imager from raspberrypi.com/software and open it.
  2. Under the operating system choice, go to Other specific‑purpose OS → Home automation → Home Assistant, and pick the build that matches your board, RPi 4 or RPi 5.
  3. Insert the microSD card and select it as storage. Everything on it will be erased.
  4. Click Next, then Write, and wait until the Imager has written and checked the card.
  5. Eject the card and put it into the Raspberry Pi.

Option C: Mini PC

A mini PC gets Home Assistant OS written onto its internal drive. The official guide for generic x86‑64 does it like this: start the mini PC from a USB stick with a live Linux system such as Ubuntu, download the Home Assistant OS image there and write it onto the internal SSD with the Disks tool or Balena Etcher. Before that, check the BIOS settings: UEFI boot must be on and Secure Boot off, or the system won't start afterwards.

Worth knowing: Writing the image erases the whole internal drive, including any Windows that came with the mini PC. Copy off anything you want to keep first.

2Step 2: First Start

  1. Connect the device to your router with the network cable.
  2. Connect the power. There is no power button on a Green or a Raspberry Pi; it starts by itself.
  3. Wait. On a Raspberry Pi 4 or 5 the system is usually ready within a few minutes; the very first start also downloads the newest version, so give it up to twenty minutes before you worry.

While you wait: no screen or keyboard is needed. Home Assistant is set up entirely from the browser on your laptop.

Worth knowing: If you want to watch the installation, connect a screen to the device's HDMI port (a micro‑HDMI cable on a Raspberry Pi 4 or 5). It shows the boot messages and, once the system is ready, a console with the device's address. You don't need it for the setup, but if the page in Step 3 won't load, the screen tells you whether the device is still busy or stuck.

3Step 3: Create Your Smart Home

On your laptop, open http://homeassistant.local:8123. If the page doesn't load, try http://homeassistant:8123, or look in your router's device list for the new device and use its address, for example http://192.168.1.10:8123.

The welcome screen offers two paths. For a new installation:

  1. Click Create my smart home. (The other path, restore, is for moving an existing installation; keep it in mind for Step 5.)
  2. Create the owner account: name, a username in lower case without spaces, and a strong password from your password manager. This account can do everything, and its password can't be recovered, so store it safely.
  3. Set your home location. Home Assistant uses it for sunrise and sunset, weather and the time zone. Search your address or click Detect, then Next.
  4. Choose what to share on the Help us help you screen. Sharing anonymous usage data is off by default; it helps the project, but it's your choice.
  5. Home Assistant shows the devices it has already found on your network, or All set! if there are none. Click Finish.

You are now looking at your own dashboard, running on a box in your home, not on someone else's server.

4Step 4: Give It a Fixed Address

Your phone, your other devices and the guides that follow need to find Home Assistant at the same address every day. Without a fixed one, the router may hand it a new address after a power cut, and suddenly nothing can reach it. IP addresses, DHCP and NAT explains why that happens.

You have two ways to fix it, and one is enough:

  • In the router: create a DHCP reservation for the Home Assistant device. The router then always gives it the same address.
  • In Home Assistant itself: under Settings → System → Network, open the network adapter, switch IPv4 from Automatic to Static, and enter an address, the subnet mask, your router as gateway and DNS server. Pick an address outside the range the router hands out automatically, so no other device ever gets it.

In this guide and the following Hands‑on articles, Home Assistant lives at 192.168.1.10.

5Step 5: Set Up Backups Right Away

A smart home grows quietly. After a few months it holds dozens of devices, automations and dashboards, and a dead microSD card would take all of it with it. Set up backups now, while there is little to lose:

  1. Open Settings → System → Backups and click Set up backups.
  2. Choose automatic backups, for example daily, and how many to keep.
  3. Choose where they go. A backup on the device itself is a start, but it dies with the device. Add a second place: a network share on your NAS, or Home Assistant Cloud if you subscribe to it.
  4. Home Assistant creates an encryption key for the backups and offers an emergency kit to download. Save it in your password manager. Without that key, nobody can restore the backup, including you.

Quick win: Test the emergency kit once: open the downloaded file and check that the key is really in your password manager. A backup you can't decrypt is no backup, as the 3‑2‑1 rule reminds us.

6Step 6: Add Your First Devices

Now the fun part. Under Settings → Devices & services, Home Assistant lists what it has discovered on the network: smart TVs, printers, speakers, sometimes the router itself. Click Add next to each one you want to use.

Devices that aren't found automatically are added with Add integration at the bottom right. Search for the brand, follow the steps, and the device appears in the list. A few practical tips for the first evening:

  • Start small. Two or three devices and one simple automation teach you more than importing everything at once.
  • Prefer local integrations. Many brands can be controlled directly on the home network. These keep working without internet and react faster than cloud ones; the integration's page tells you which kind it is.
  • For Zigbee and Thread, Home Assistant needs a radio. The Green and the Raspberry Pi have none built in, so you add a USB stick. Plug it in with a short USB extension cable, away from the device and from USB 3 ports, because those disturb the 2.4 GHz radio, the same effect that makes wireless mice lag.

Keep It Safe

Home Assistant now knows when you are home and can open your garage. Treat it accordingly:

  • Install updates when Home Assistant offers them under Settings. Read the short release notes for anything that affects your devices, and let the automatic backup run before larger updates.
  • Don't open ports on the router to reach it from outside. That is the shortcut that puts it on every scanner's list; reaching your network safely shows the better ways.
  • Separate the gadgets. Cheap smart plugs and cameras rarely get updates. A separate network for them, as in segmenting your home network, keeps them away from your laptops.
  • One account per person. Create users for the rest of the household under Settings → People, instead of sharing the owner account.

For Small Businesses

Home Assistant isn't building management software, but it fits small offices and shops well: it switches lights and heating by opening hours, sends a message when the server room gets too warm, or tells you when the fridge in the kitchenette is left open. Put it on its own device, protect it with a UPS if it watches anything important, and document who has the owner password and where the emergency kit is stored.

Conclusion

Five apps for five brands, and devices that die with their manufacturer's cloud: Home Assistant ends that in one evening. Choose Home Assistant OS on its own small device, a Green for the easiest start or a Raspberry Pi or mini PC if you have one, write the image, connect it with a cable and create your smart home at homeassistant.local:8123. Then give it a fixed address, set up automatic backups with the emergency kit in your password manager, and add your first devices one by one. Everything is now under one roof, your own, and ready for the next step: an ad filter for the whole home.
