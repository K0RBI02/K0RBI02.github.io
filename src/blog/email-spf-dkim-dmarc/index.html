SPF, DKIM, DMARC: Making Your Business Email Trustworthy

Why emails from your own domain land in spam, how forged emails in your name get through, and the three DNS records that fix both.

An invoice to a customer ends up in their spam folder. A supplier receives a payment request "from you" that you never sent. Both problems have the same root: the recipient's mail server cannot tell whether an email claiming to come from your domain really does. Forged emails like that are how many phishing attacks begin.

The good news: three DNS records answer exactly that question. They take an afternoon to set up, and the large email providers increasingly expect them.

The Problem: Anyone Can Write Your Name on the Envelope

Email was designed in a more trusting time. The sender address is just text, and any server can claim to send mail for example.com. Three standards fix this, each covering a different gap:

Record Question it answers How
SPF Is this server allowed to send for the domain? A list of permitted senders in DNS
DKIM Was the email changed on the way, and who signed it? A cryptographic signature, the key published in DNS
DMARC What should happen if the checks fail, and who gets reports? A policy in DNS

SPF: The Guest List

The SPF record lists the servers that may send email for your domain. It is a TXT record on the domain itself:

v=spf1 include:spf.protection.outlook.com include:_spf.example-newsletter.com -all

Each include adds the servers of one service, for example your email provider and your newsletter tool. The -all at the end means "everyone else is not allowed".

  • One SPF record per domain. Two records are an error, so combine everything into one.
  • Mind the limit of ten lookups. Each include counts. Long lists of services can exceed it and break SPF entirely.
  • Forwarding breaks SPF. When a recipient forwards your email, it arrives from their server, not yours. That is why SPF alone is not enough.

DKIM: The Seal on the Envelope

With DKIM, your email provider signs every outgoing message with a private key. The matching public key is published in DNS under a selector, for example selector1._domainkey.example.com. The receiving server checks the signature and knows that the email really came through your provider and was not changed on the way.

DKIM survives forwarding, which makes it the more robust of the two. Most providers, such as Microsoft 365 and Google Workspace, generate the keys for you; you only add the DNS records they show and switch signing on.

DMARC: The House Rules

DMARC ties SPF and DKIM to the address people actually see in the From line, and tells receivers what to do when an email fails:

v=DMARC1; p=none; rua=mailto:dmarc-reports@example.com

This TXT record lives at _dmarc.example.com. The policy p has three levels:

  1. p=none: only observe and send reports. Start here.
  2. p=quarantine: failing emails go to spam.
  3. p=reject: failing emails are refused. This is the goal, because only then can nobody forge your domain.

The reports sent to the rua address show which servers send email in your name, both legitimate ones you forgot and forgers. They arrive as XML files, so a free or inexpensive report service that turns them into readable overviews is worth it.

The Rollout That Works

  1. List every service that sends email for your domain. Not just the mailbox provider: the newsletter tool, the online shop, the accounting software, the booking system, the website's contact form, the scanner that sends PDFs.
  2. Set up SPF and DKIM for each of them. Most services have instructions for exactly this.
  3. Publish DMARC with p=none and read the reports for two to four weeks.
  4. Fix what the reports show, then move to p=quarantine and later to p=reject.

Since 2024, Google and Yahoo require authentication from everyone who sends to their users, and DMARC from senders of large volumes. Other providers follow the same direction, so this is quickly becoming the minimum for reliable delivery.

Domains That Never Send Email

Many businesses own extra domains, old names, typo variants or campaign domains. Attackers like these for forgery precisely because nobody watches them. Protect them with two short records: Whoever controls the registrar account controls these domains as well, so protect it with two‑factor authentication and keep the domains under the business's control.

  • SPF: v=spf1 -all (nobody may send)
  • DMARC: v=DMARC1; p=reject;

Conclusion

SPF lists who may send, DKIM proves an email is genuine and unchanged, DMARC decides what happens when the checks fail. Together they keep your invoices out of spam folders and make it much harder to send fake emails in your name. Start by listing every sending service, set up SPF and DKIM, observe with DMARC for a few weeks, then tighten it to reject. All three are DNS records, which makes this a good moment to read up on how DNS works.
