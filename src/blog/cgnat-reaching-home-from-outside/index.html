Can't Reach Home From Outside? CGNAT, Public IPv4 and Dynamic DNS

Why port forwarding sometimes does nothing at all, how to find out if your line is shared, and the ways around it.

You set up the camera, the NAS or the VPN server, add the port forwarding in the router, and it works perfectly at home. Then you try it from your phone on mobile data, and nothing happens. No error, just a timeout. The router settings look right, however often you check them.

The good news: this is rarely a mistake on your side, and once you know what your provider is doing with your address, the fix is usually straightforward.

Your Address Is Probably Not Your Own

To reach something at home from outside, the outside world needs an address that leads to your router. With IPv4, that address is the public IPv4 address of your connection. Inside your home, devices use private addresses (192.168.x.x and similar), and the router translates between the two. That translation is called NAT, and port forwarding is how you tell the router which inside device should receive a particular incoming connection.

The catch: IPv4 addresses ran out years ago, so many providers no longer give each connection its own. Instead, they do the same trick your router does, one level higher.

CGNAT

With carrier-grade NAT (CGNAT), the provider puts many customers behind one public IPv4 address. Your router gets an address from the provider's internal network, often from the range 100.64.0.0/10 that was reserved for exactly this purpose. Outgoing connections work as usual: browsing, streaming and calls are unaffected.

Incoming connections are a different story. When a packet arrives at the shared public address, the provider's NAT has no idea which of its hundreds of customers it is meant for, so it drops it. Your port forwarding never even sees it.

DS‑Lite

DS‑Lite (Dual‑Stack Lite) is a common variant, especially on cable connections. Your router gets a proper public IPv6 prefix, but no public IPv4 address at all. IPv4 traffic travels through a tunnel to the provider, where it is translated on a shared address. The result for incoming IPv4 is the same as with CGNAT: it cannot reach you. IPv6, on the other hand, works fully, which matters later.

Mobile and 5G home internet connections are very often behind CGNAT too. Fibre and DSL lines are more likely to come with a public IPv4 address, but it varies by provider and tariff.

How to Check Your Own Line

It takes two minutes.

  1. Open the router's status page and look for the internet address or WAN IPv4 address.
  2. On a device at home, open any "what is my IP" website and note the IPv4 address it shows.
  3. Compare them.
What you see What it means Verdict
Both addresses are the same You have your own public IPv4 address Port forwarding and dynamic DNS will work
Router shows 100.64.x.x to 100.127.x.x CGNAT Incoming IPv4 is impossible; see the options below
Router shows 10.x.x.x, 172.16–31.x.x or 192.168.x.x Another NAT in front of you, at the provider or in your own home Check for a second router first (double NAT)
Router shows no IPv4, or mentions "DS‑Lite" DS‑Lite IPv4 cannot come in; IPv6 can

Worth knowing: If the router reports a private address, the culprit is sometimes in your own home: a provider modem‑router with your own router behind it. That is called double NAT, and you can fix it yourself by switching the first box to bridge or modem mode, or by forwarding everything to your router (often called exposed host).

Dynamic DNS: Useful, but Not a Cure

Even with a public IPv4 address, there is a second hurdle. Most home connections get a dynamic address that can change after a reconnect, a router restart or simply over time.

Dynamic DNS (DDNS) solves that. You get a name such as myhome.example.com, and the router, or a small program on a device at home, tells the DDNS service every time the address changes. Many routers have this built in; FRITZ!Box routers, for example, include AVM's own MyFRITZ! service. If you want to know how names turn into addresses in general, see DNS explained.

What DDNS cannot do is give you an address you do not have. Behind CGNAT, the router would dutifully publish its 100.64.x.x address or the provider's shared one, and neither leads back to you.

Rule of thumb: Dynamic DNS fixes a changing address. It does nothing for a shared one.

Your Options Behind CGNAT

Ask for a Public IPv4 Address

The simplest route is often a phone call. Some providers will switch your line to a public IPv4 address on request, sometimes free, sometimes for a monthly fee, and often as part of a business tariff, which may also include a static address that never changes. Ask specifically for "a public IPv4 address" or "dual stack instead of DS‑Lite", so the hotline knows what you mean.

Use IPv6

If your line has IPv6, every device at home already has a globally reachable address. You open a firewall rule on the router for that one device and port, and it can be reached directly, without any NAT in between. Our article on IPv6 at home and in the office explains the basics.

Two catches. First, the network you are connecting from needs IPv6 as well. Many mobile networks offer it today, but hotel and café Wi‑Fi often does not, one more reason to use your phone's hotspot on the road. Second, if you use dynamic DNS with IPv6, the name must point to the device's IPv6 address, not the router's, and many IPv6 addresses change over time as well. Some routers handle this for you; otherwise a small update client on the device itself does the job.

Connect Outwards Instead

All of the remaining options share one clever idea: if nothing can get in, let the inside connect out.

  • Overlay VPNs such as Tailscale or ZeroTier connect your devices into a private network. Each device dials out to a coordination service, and the devices then talk to each other directly and encrypted wherever possible. No port forwarding, no public address needed.
  • Tunnel services such as Cloudflare Tunnel run a small connector at home that keeps an outgoing connection open. They are made for publishing a website or web app, not for reaching your whole network.
  • A small rented server (a VPS) with its own public address can act as the meeting point: your home connects to it with WireGuard, and you connect to it from outside. More work, but entirely under your control.

Each of these moves a piece of trust to someone else's infrastructure, so pick a provider deliberately and protect the accounts with two‑factor authentication.

Which Way Should You Go?

  • You only want to reach your own things (NAS, smart home, cameras): an overlay VPN is the fastest working solution and avoids open ports altogether.
  • You want a classic VPN server on your router: ask the provider for a public IPv4 address, then add dynamic DNS.
  • You host something for others (a website, a shared calendar): a tunnel service or a public IPv4 address with a reverse proxy and HTTPS in front.

Whatever you choose, do not simply expose a device's login page to the internet. The safer patterns are described in reaching your network safely.

For Small Businesses

An office that needs to be reached from outside, for a VPN, a phone system or a camera system, should not depend on a residential line's goodwill. Business tariffs usually include one or more static public IPv4 addresses, and that alone removes CGNAT and dynamic DNS from the picture. Put it on the checklist when you compare offers, alongside upload and repair times. A 5G backup line is most likely behind CGNAT, so test what still works when the main line is down.

Conclusion

When remote access fails without an error, check whether your router's internet address matches the one the world sees. If it does, add dynamic DNS and you are done. If it starts with 100.64 to 100.127, or your line runs DS‑Lite, incoming IPv4 cannot reach you: ask the provider for a public address, use IPv6, or let your home connect outwards with an overlay VPN or tunnel. And whichever door you open, put a VPN or strong authentication in front of it.
