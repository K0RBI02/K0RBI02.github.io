Port Forwarding, VPN, Zero Trust: Reaching Your Network Safely

Remote access is one of the most common ways attackers get in. Here is how to do it right.

Sooner or later, everyone wants to reach something at home or in the office from outside: the NAS with the family photos, the security camera, the accounting software, a file server. The quickest way to make that work is also the most dangerous one.

The Problem With Port Forwarding

Port forwarding tells the router: "send any connection that arrives on this port straight to this device inside". It works instantly — and it puts that device directly on the internet. And on many lines it does not work at all, because the provider shares one address among many customers: see CGNAT.

Within minutes of being exposed, a service is found by automated scanners that continuously sweep the entire IPv4 address space. From then on it faces a steady stream of login attempts and exploit attempts against known vulnerabilities. Exposed remote desktop (RDP) servers in particular have been one of the most common entry points for ransomware attacks on businesses.

Rule of thumb: If a device's login page is reachable from the internet, assume someone is trying passwords on it right now. Strong, unique passwords and two‑factor authentication are the minimum, but no substitute for keeping it off the internet.

Option 1: A VPN Into Your Network

A VPN (virtual private network) creates an encrypted tunnel from your device into your network. Only the VPN itself is reachable from outside; everything else stays hidden behind it.

  • WireGuard is modern, fast and lean, and supported by many routers and firewalls. Access is based on cryptographic keys rather than passwords. Many routers have it built in, one more reason to keep the router itself up to date.
  • IPsec and OpenVPN are established alternatives, widely supported in business equipment.

Many home routers include a VPN server that takes a few minutes to set up. For businesses, the firewall typically provides it. On the road, this is also the better alternative to a commercial VPN app, as explained in internet on the road.

Worth knowing: VPN appliances at the network edge have themselves become a favourite target in recent years. Whatever device runs your VPN must be patched promptly — it is the front door.

Option 2: Overlay Networks

Mesh VPN services connect your devices into a private network that works across locations without opening any incoming ports. Each device connects outward to a coordination service, and the devices then talk to each other directly and encrypted.

This is especially convenient behind CGNAT or DS‑Lite, where incoming IPv4 connections are not possible at all. The trade-off is that you depend on a third-party service for coordination, so check its security model and where it is hosted.

Option 3: Zero Trust Access

Traditional VPNs follow a castle-and-moat idea: once inside, you can reach a lot. Zero trust network access (ZTNA) turns this around:

  • Every connection to every application is checked individually.
  • Identity, device health and context decide who may reach what.
  • Users see only the applications they are allowed to use, never the network itself.

For companies with remote staff and cloud applications, ZTNA increasingly replaces the classic VPN. For a household it is usually more than needed.

Whatever You Choose

  1. Multi-factor authentication on every remote login. A stolen password alone should never be enough.
  2. Least privilege: give each person access only to what they need.
  3. Updates: router, firewall, VPN server and the devices behind them.
  4. Logging: know who connected, when and from where — and look at it occasionally.
  5. Close what you no longer use. Old port forwardings and forgotten accounts are classic weak spots.

Conclusion

Port forwarding is the quick fix that tends to become a permanent risk. A VPN is the solid default for homes and small businesses, overlay networks make life easier behind shared IP addresses, and zero trust is where larger organisations are heading. Whatever route you take, put multi-factor authentication in front of it and keep the front door patched.
