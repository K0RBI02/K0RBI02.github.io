15:T15e5,<p>Small businesses often run on a network that began as a consumer router and a few switches and simply kept growing. It works — until it doesn't. When the internet goes down, the card terminal stops, the phones fall silent and nobody knows which cable goes where.</p>
<p>You do not need a data centre to avoid this. You need a handful of decisions made deliberately.</p>
<h2 id="1-a-second-way-out">1. A Second Way Out</h2>
<p>If the business stops when the internet stops, a single line is a single point of failure.</p>
<ul>
<li>Use a router or firewall with <strong>dual-WAN failover</strong>, which switches to a backup line automatically.</li>
<li>Choose a backup of a <strong>different technology</strong> — for example 5G as a backup for fiber. A digger cutting a cable in the street will take out two fixed lines in the same trench just as easily as one.</li>
<li>Test the failover regularly: unplug the main line and check that phones, payments and cloud services keep working.</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Worth knowing:</strong> Business tariffs often include a guaranteed repair time. For many companies this matters more than the advertised speed.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="2-separate-networks-for-separate-purposes">2. Separate Networks for Separate Purposes</h2>
<p>Not everything belongs in one network. A typical small office needs at least:</p>
<div class="table-wrap"><table>
<thead>
<tr>
<th>Network</th>
<th>Purpose</th>
</tr>
</thead>
<tbody>
<tr>
<td>Staff</td>
<td>Work computers and servers</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Voice</td>
<td>IP phones, with priority for call quality</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Payments</td>
<td>Card terminals and point of sale, as isolated as possible</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Devices</td>
<td>Printers, cameras, building technology</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Guests</td>
<td>Visitor and customer Wi‑Fi, internet only</td>
</tr>
</tbody>
</table></div>
<p>With <a href="/src/blog/vlans-managed-switches/">managed switches and VLANs</a>, all of these can share the same cabling while remaining logically separate. A guest's infected laptop then never gets close to the accounting PC.</p>
<h2 id="3-cabling-and-power-done-properly">3. Cabling and Power Done Properly</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Use cable wherever devices do not move.</strong> Desktops, printers, phones and access points belong on Ethernet. Cat 6 or Cat 6A is a sensible choice for new installations.</li>
<li><strong>Plan PoE (Power over Ethernet).</strong> Access points, phones and cameras can be powered through the network cable. Check that the switch has enough total PoE budget for all of them. And keep the network corner on a <a href="/src/blog/ups-for-your-network/">UPS</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Add a UPS.</strong> A small uninterruptible power supply for the router, the fiber terminal and the core switch bridges short power cuts and protects against surges. Without it, every flicker means a full restart of the network. If servers are involved too, read <a href="/src/blog/closet-rack-colocation-or-cloud/">closet rack, colocation or cloud</a> before deciding where they should live.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="4-professional-wifi-instead-of-a-single-router">4. Professional Wi‑Fi Instead of a Single Router</h2>
<p>One router in the corner is rarely enough for an office. Several <strong>ceiling-mounted access points</strong>, cabled and centrally managed, give even coverage and let devices roam smoothly between rooms. A short site survey — walking the premises with a measuring app — shows where coverage is weak before staff start complaining.</p>
<h2 id="5-security-as-a-routine-not-a-project">5. Security as a Routine, Not a Project</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Firmware updates</strong> for the firewall, switches and access points on a fixed schedule.</li>
<li><strong>No exposed admin interfaces</strong> or remote desktop ports on the internet. Use a VPN with multi-factor authentication for remote access. And remove access the day someone leaves, see <a href="/src/blog/onboarding-offboarding/">hello and goodbye</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Strong, unique passwords</strong> for every network device, stored in a password manager.</li>
<li><strong>Backups</strong> that <a href="/src/blog/backups-3-2-1/">follow the 3‑2‑1 rule</a>: three copies, on two different media, one of them off-site — and at least one that ransomware cannot reach.</li>
</ul>
<p>Depending on your sector and size, regulation may also apply. In the EU, the NIS2 directive brings formal security requirements for many medium-sized and larger companies; it is worth checking whether your business falls under it.</p>
<h2 id="6-documentation">6. Documentation</h2>
<p>The most underrated part of any network is a simple, current document that answers:</p>
<ul>
<li>Which lines, contracts and customer numbers do we have — and who do we call when one fails?</li>
<li>Which devices exist, where are they, and how do we log in?</li>
<li>Which switch port leads to which socket?</li>
<li>Which networks (VLANs) exist and what are they for?</li>
</ul>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Quick win:</strong> Label both ends of every cable and keep the network plan where a stranger could find it in an emergency. The person fixing the outage is often not the person who built the network.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Reliable small business networks are not about the most expensive equipment. They come from a second internet line, clear separation, proper cabling and power, managed Wi‑Fi, routine security and a plan someone else can read.
