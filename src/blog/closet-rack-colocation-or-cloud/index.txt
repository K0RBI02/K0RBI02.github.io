15:T2326,<p>The server sits in the storage room, next to the cleaning supplies. It has done so for years. Then comes a hot week in July, the room reaches 35 °C, and the machine shuts itself down in the middle of the working day. Or the cleaner needs a socket for the vacuum. Or the office line goes down and nobody, inside or outside, can reach the files that matter.</p>
<p>The good news: you have more options than "keep it in the closet" or "move everything to the cloud", and the right one usually becomes obvious once you know what each place really provides.</p>
<h2 id="what-a-data-centre-actually-does">What a Data Centre Actually Does</h2>
<p>A data centre is, at its heart, a building that sells four things your office cannot easily provide.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Power that does not stop.</strong> Large battery systems bridge the first minutes of an outage, diesel generators take over after that, and racks are usually fed by two independent power paths (an A and a B feed). A server with two power supplies can lose either path without noticing.</li>
<li><strong>Cooling.</strong> Air conditioning with spare capacity keeps temperature and humidity in a narrow range, all year round, with redundant units so that one can be serviced while the others run.</li>
<li><strong>Physical security.</strong> Access control, cameras, logged entry, locked cages or racks, and staff on site around the clock. Fire detection reacts early, and suppression systems are designed to protect the equipment.</li>
<li><strong>Connectivity.</strong> Many data centres are <em>carrier-neutral</em>: several network providers are present in the building, and you choose one or more. Some host internet exchange points too. In Vienna, for example, the VIX exchange operates one of its sites in a data centre campus in the north of the city. As described in <a href="/src/blog/how-the-internet-backbone-works/">how the internet backbone works</a>, being close to these meeting points means short and reliable paths.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="tiers-in-one-paragraph">Tiers in One Paragraph</h3>
<p>You will often see data centres described as <strong>Tier I to Tier IV</strong>, a classification from the Uptime Institute. Tier I has basic infrastructure with no redundancy. Tier III is <em>concurrently maintainable</em>: any part can be serviced without switching off your equipment. Tier IV is <em>fault tolerant</em>: it keeps running through an unplanned failure of any single component. The design availability rises from about 99.67 % (Tier I) to 99.98 % (Tier III) and 99.995 % (Tier IV). Treat these as design targets, not promises, and check whether a facility is actually certified or only "built to Tier III standards". In Europe, the standard EN 50600 plays a similar role.</p>
<h2 id="three-places-for-your-servers">Three Places for Your Servers</h2>
<h3 id="the-closet-rack">The Closet Rack</h3>
<p>A small rack in the office: a switch, a firewall, perhaps a NAS and a server. It is close, cheap and fully under your control. You can walk over and press a button.</p>
<p>It also inherits every weakness of the building: the office internet line, the office power, the office climate and whoever has a key. With some care, these can be managed:</p>
<ul>
<li>A lockable rack or room, not a shared storage cupboard.</li>
<li>Ventilation or a small air conditioner if the room gets warm; heat is the most underestimated enemy.</li>
<li>A <a href="/src/blog/ups-for-your-network/">UPS</a> that keeps things running through short outages and shuts servers down cleanly in long ones.</li>
<li><a href="/src/blog/monitoring-small-networks/">Monitoring</a> that tells you about a failed disk or a hot room before the users do.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="colocation">Colocation</h3>
<p>With <strong>colocation</strong>, you rent space in a data centre and bring your own hardware. Space is sold by height units (one unit, or 1U, is 44.45 mm), by quarter, half or full rack, or by caged areas. Power is usually charged separately, by contracted capacity or measured consumption, and connectivity is booked from the providers in the building.</p>
<p>The data centre looks after the building; everything inside your rack remains yours: hardware, operating systems, updates, backups. For tasks that need hands on the hardware, such as swapping a disk or pressing a power button, most facilities offer <strong>remote hands</strong>, billed by time.</p>
<p>Colocation makes sense when:</p>
<ul>
<li>you own hardware that is worth running for years, with a steady, predictable load,</li>
<li>you need a better internet connection or uptime than the office can offer,</li>
<li>you want your data on your own machines but not in your own building.</li>
</ul>
<h3 id="the-cloud">The Cloud</h3>
<p>With the cloud you rent capacity instead of owning hardware. It comes in two flavours that are worth keeping apart: The other building blocks of a reliable office are in <a href="/src/blog/small-business-network-essentials/">six things that actually matter</a>.</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Infrastructure (IaaS):</strong> virtual servers, storage and networks you configure yourself. Fast to start, easy to resize, billed by usage. You still manage the operating system and everything above it, as with <a href="/src/blog/vms-and-containers/">VMs and containers</a> at home.</li>
<li><strong>Software as a service (SaaS):</strong> finished applications such as email, office suites, accounting or file sharing. The provider runs everything; you manage users, settings and data.</li>
</ul>
<p>For many small businesses, the real move is not "server to cloud server" but "server to SaaS": the file server becomes a cloud file service, the mail server becomes hosted mail, and the server in the closet simply has nothing left to do.</p>

<h2 id="questions-before-you-sign">Questions Before You Sign</h2>
<p>Whether you choose colocation or a cloud provider, a few questions separate a good contract from an expensive surprise:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Where is the data?</strong> For personal data, an EU location and a data processing agreement under the GDPR are the baseline.</li>
<li><strong>How do you get in?</strong> For colocation: 24/7 access, how far the drive is, and what remote hands cost per hour.</li>
<li><strong>What is in the SLA?</strong> Availability, response times and what you get back if they are missed, which is usually a credit, not compensation for your losses.</li>
<li><strong>Which certifications?</strong> ISO/IEC 27001 for information security is a reasonable minimum to ask about.</li>
<li><strong>How do you leave?</strong> Export formats, notice periods and who deletes what. Moving out should be planned on the day you move in.</li>
</ol>
<p>If your company falls under Austria's NIS2 implementation (the NISG 2026, in force since October 2026), supplier security is part of your own obligations. Ask your hosting and cloud providers for the documentation you will need.</p>
<h2 id="what-does-not-change">What Does Not Change</h2>
<p>No location removes the need for a backup. A data centre protects against fire in your office, not against a deleted folder, ransomware or a mistake in the cloud console. Keep an independent copy elsewhere, as described in <a href="/src/blog/backups-3-2-1/">the 3‑2‑1 rule</a>, and test the restore.</p>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>A data centre sells uninterrupted power, cooling, physical security and good connectivity; a closet can only approximate them. For most small businesses, the sensible split is SaaS for standard office tasks, a lockable, ventilated closet with a UPS for the network equipment, and colocation only for hardware you want to keep running yourself.
