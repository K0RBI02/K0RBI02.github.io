15:T2527,<p>The tablet in the kitchen is nine years old and still perfect for recipes. The NAS in the hallway has been running since 2017 without a single complaint. The laptop from the last job is "fine for emails". None of them has received a security update in years, and nobody in the house would know where to check. Then the banking app stops opening on the tablet, and a message appears on the laptop that something is "no longer supported".</p>
<p>The good news: you do not have to replace everything at once. Knowing which devices are still supported, and handling the others deliberately, removes most of the risk.</p>
<h2 id="why-updates-matter-more-than-antivirus">Why Updates Matter More Than Antivirus</h2>
<p>Most successful attacks do not use clever new tricks. They use <strong>known flaws</strong> that the manufacturer fixed months or years ago, on devices that never installed the fix. Once a fix is published, attackers can study it to see exactly what was broken, and automated scans look for every device on the internet that still has the old version.</p>
<p>That is why one rule protects more than any extra security product: <strong>keep everything updated, automatically where possible.</strong></p>
<h2 id="what-end-of-support-really-means">What "End of Support" Really Means</h2>
<p>A device that reaches end of support keeps working. Nothing switches off. What stops are the fixes: every flaw discovered from that day on stays open for good. Over time, other things follow:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Apps stop supporting the old system.</strong> Banking apps, browsers and messengers usually go first, because they care most about security.</li>
<li><strong>Websites and services refuse old browsers</strong> that lack current encryption.</li>
<li><strong>Known flaws pile up</strong> and become part of standard attack tools.</li>
</ul>
<p>The risk depends on what the device does. An old e‑book reader that only downloads books is a small problem. An old router that sits between your whole network and the internet, or a NAS full of family photos, is a big one.</p>
<h2 id="how-long-is-your-device-supported">How Long Is Your Device Supported?</h2>
<p>Manufacturers handle this very differently. Some publish end‑of‑life lists, others say nothing at all.</p>

<p><strong>Quick win:</strong> Walk through the house once and note every device with its last update date. Anything older than a year without updates goes on a list for the next steps.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="turn-on-automatic-updates">Turn On Automatic Updates</h2>
<p>Updates only help when they are installed, and manual updates get postponed forever. Turn on automatic installation wherever it exists:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Computers:</strong> Windows Update and macOS Software Update, including app updates from the app stores.</li>
<li><strong>Phones and tablets:</strong> automatic system and app updates, ideally over Wi‑Fi at night.</li>
<li><strong>Router and mesh system:</strong> automatic firmware updates, as described in the <a href="/src/blog/router-hardening/">router security checklist</a>.</li>
<li><strong>NAS and home server:</strong> automatic updates for the system and the apps on it. A <a href="/src/blog/first-home-server/">home server</a> with containers needs this just as much.</li>
<li><strong>Smart home:</strong> firmware updates in the manufacturer's app or the hub.</li>
</ul>
<p>Big upgrades, such as a new operating system version, are the exception: make sure you have a working <a href="/src/blog/backups-3-2-1/">backup</a> first, then install them within a few weeks rather than on day one.</p>
<h2 id="windows-10-a-special-case">Windows 10: A Special Case</h2>
<p>Windows 10 left regular support in October 2025. Microsoft then offered home users an extension called <strong>Extended Security Updates (ESU)</strong>: security fixes only, no new features. According to Microsoft's ESU page, the consumer programme now runs until <strong>12 October 2027</strong>, and devices that are already enrolled stay covered automatically. You enrol in Windows Update, need Windows 10 version 22H2 and must sign in with a Microsoft account.</p>
<p>Treat ESU as a bridge, not a destination. Use the time to check whether the PC can run Windows 11, or plan its replacement. Older PCs that cannot upgrade often still run a lightweight Linux system well, which is a fine option for a second computer that mainly needs a browser.</p>
<h2 id="when-a-device-is-out-of-support">When a Device Is Out of Support</h2>
<p>Replacing it is the cleanest answer, but not always the only one. Four options, from strongest to weakest:</p>
<ol>
<li><strong>Replace it.</strong> For the router and anything that is reachable from the internet, this is the right answer. Old routers are a favourite target for automated attacks.</li>
<li><strong>Give it new software.</strong> Some devices accept maintained alternatives: many routers run OpenWrt, an open‑source router system, and old PCs run Linux. This takes some tinkering and is not possible on every model.</li>
<li><strong>Isolate it.</strong> Put old smart TVs, cameras and gadgets on a guest or IoT network, so they cannot reach your laptop or NAS. Our guide to <a href="/src/blog/segmenting-your-home-network/">segmenting your home network</a> shows how.</li>
<li><strong>Reduce what it can do.</strong> No port forwarding to it, no remote access from the internet; if you need to reach it from outside, use <a href="/src/blog/secure-remote-access/">a safe way to reach your network</a>. Remove accounts and data it no longer needs.</li>
</ol>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> The more a device is exposed and the more it knows, the sooner it must be replaced. Router and NAS first, the kitchen radio last.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="what-the-eu-is-changing">What the EU Is Changing</h2>
<p>Buying devices with a clear support promise is getting easier. New smartphones and tablets sold in the EU since June 2025 must receive updates for years after the model leaves the shops. Since August 2025, new wireless devices also have to meet basic security requirements. The bigger step is the <strong>Cyber Resilience Act</strong>: from December 2027, manufacturers of most connected products must state at purchase how long the product will receive security updates, normally at least five years, and deliver them for that time. Until then, check the support period yourself before you buy, and prefer brands that publish it.</p>
<h2 id="for-small-businesses">For Small Businesses</h2>
<p>In an office, an unsupported device is not only a risk but also a question of due care. A few habits keep it under control:</p>
<ul>
<li><strong>Keep an inventory</strong> with purchase dates and end‑of‑support dates for computers, phones, router, firewall, switches, NAS and printers. The same list helps with <a href="/src/blog/onboarding-offboarding/">onboarding and offboarding</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Manage updates centrally.</strong> Device management tools install updates and show which devices are behind.</li>
<li><strong>Budget replacements</strong> on a fixed cycle instead of waiting for failures, typically four to six years for computers.</li>
<li><strong>Check specialist software.</strong> Accounting, medical or industrial software sometimes ties you to old systems. Ask the vendor about their plans early and keep those machines separated.</li>
<li><strong>Windows 10 in a business:</strong> PCs joined to a domain, Microsoft Entra ID or a device management system cannot use the consumer extension. Businesses buy ESU per device instead, for a limited number of years.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>Old devices are not dangerous because they are old, but because nobody fixes them anymore. Turn on automatic updates everywhere, find out which devices have reached end of support, and deal with each one deliberately: replace the router and anything exposed to the internet, give old computers new software or a new job, and move old gadgets onto a separate network.
