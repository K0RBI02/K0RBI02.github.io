15:T1536,<p>The email looked exactly like the parcel service, the bank or the company's own IT department. You clicked, entered your password, maybe opened an attachment, and a moment later something feels wrong. The first reaction is often embarrassment and the urge to hope for the best.</p>
<p>The good news: acting quickly limits the damage enormously. Phishing works because it targets everyone; reporting it fast is what separates a scare from a disaster.</p>
<h2 id="first-what-exactly-happened">First: What Exactly Happened?</h2>
<p>The right steps depend on what you did:</p>

<td>Entered a password</td>
<td>High</td>
<td>Step 1</td>
</tr>
<tr>
<td>Opened an attachment or installed something</td>
<td>High</td>
<td>Step 2</td>
</tr>

</tr>

<h2 id="step-1-a-password-was-entered">Step 1: A Password Was Entered</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Change the password immediately</strong>, preferably from a different device. If you used the same password elsewhere, change it there too.</li>
<li><strong>Sign out all sessions.</strong> Most services have an option like "sign out everywhere". Attackers often stay logged in even after a password change.</li>
<li><strong>Check the two‑factor settings and recovery options.</strong> Remove any phone number, app or email address you do not recognise.</li>
<li><strong>Check the mailbox for new rules.</strong> Attackers like to add forwarding rules or filters that hide replies. Remove anything unfamiliar.</li>
</ol>
<p>A password manager and passkeys make this much less likely next time (<a href="/src/blog/passwords-passkeys-2fa/">why</a>).</p>
<h2 id="step-2-something-was-opened-or-installed">Step 2: Something Was Opened or Installed</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Disconnect the device from the network</strong>: unplug the cable, switch off Wi‑Fi. Leave it switched on, so IT can see what happened. If ransomware is involved, a good <a href="/src/blog/backups-3-2-1/">backup</a> is what brings the data back.</li>
<li><strong>Tell IT or your IT service provider at once.</strong> At home, ask someone who knows computers to help, or a repair service you trust.</li>
<li><strong>Do not log in to other accounts on that device</strong> until it has been checked or reinstalled.</li>
<li><strong>Change important passwords from a clean device</strong>, starting with email and banking.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="step-3-money-or-card-details-are-involved">Step 3: Money or Card Details Are Involved</h2>
<ol>
<li><strong>Call your bank immediately</strong> and block the card. In Austria, debit cards can be blocked around the clock via the card blocking hotline printed on the back of your card and on your bank's website.</li>
<li><strong>Ask the bank to recall transfers.</strong> The sooner they know, the better the chances.</li>
<li><strong>Keep all evidence</strong>: emails, screenshots, transaction details. Do not delete the phishing email yet.</li>
</ol>
<h2 id="who-to-tell">Who to Tell</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>The police</strong>: fraud and cybercrime can be reported at any police station; in Austria the Federal Criminal Police Office also runs a reporting point for internet crime.</li>
<li><strong>Watchlist Internet</strong> collects reports of current scams and warns others, which helps the next person.</li>
<li><strong>Colleagues</strong>, if it happened at work: the same email has probably reached others. A quick warning stops the next click.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="extra-duties-for-businesses">Extra Duties for Businesses</h2>
<p>If personal data of customers or employees may have been exposed, the GDPR applies: a data breach generally has to be reported to the data protection authority within <strong>72 hours</strong> of discovering it, and in serious cases the affected people must be informed. Document what happened and when. Businesses that fall under the NIS2 rules may have additional reporting duties.</p>
<blockquote>
<p><strong>Rule of thumb:</strong> In a business, nobody should be afraid to report a click. A colleague who reports within five minutes is worth more than one who stays silent out of embarrassment.</p>
</blockquote>
<h2 id="after-the-first-hour">After the First Hour</h2>
<ul>
<li><strong>Watch the affected accounts and bank statements</strong> closely for a few weeks.</li>
<li><strong>Expect follow‑up attempts:</strong> attackers often call or write again, pretending to help with "the security incident".</li>
<li><strong>Learn from it</strong> without blame: what made the email convincing, and what would have helped spot it? For businesses, <a href="/src/blog/email-spf-dkim-dmarc/">SPF, DKIM and DMARC</a> make forged emails in your own name much harder to send.</li>
</ul>
<h2 id="conclusion">Conclusion</h2>
<p>When phishing succeeds, speed matters more than anything else. Change the password and sign out everywhere, disconnect an infected device, call the bank if money is involved, and tell the people who need to know.
