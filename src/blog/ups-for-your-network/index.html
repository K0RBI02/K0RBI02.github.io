A One‑Second Power Cut, a Ten‑Minute Outage: Why Networks Need a UPS

Routers, NAS and phone systems hate unstable power. A small uninterruptible power supply fixes more than you would expect.

The lights flicker for a fraction of a second. Nobody in the room even notices, but the router, the fibre modem and the switch restart. Getting the connection back takes several minutes, the video call drops, the card terminal in the shop goes offline and the NAS runs a long consistency check. A power cut you barely saw has turned into a ten‑minute outage.

The good news: protecting the network against this is cheap, because network equipment needs very little power.

What Goes Wrong With Power

Not every power problem is a blackout:

  • Short dips: a few hundred milliseconds of low voltage, for example when a large machine starts. Enough to reboot a router.
  • Outages: from seconds to hours, after storms, construction work or a tripped fuse.
  • Surges: short voltage peaks, for example from nearby lightning strikes, which can damage power supplies and network ports.

Routers and modems rarely break from this, but every restart costs minutes. A NAS or server that loses power mid‑write risks corrupted files and can take hours to check its disks.

What a UPS Does

An uninterruptible power supply (UPS) sits between the wall socket and your equipment. A battery inside takes over within milliseconds when the mains power fails. There are three designs:

Type How it works Verdict
Standby (offline) Switches to battery when power fails Cheap; fine for routers and modems
Line‑interactive Also corrects small voltage dips without using the battery The sweet spot for networks and small servers
Online (double conversion) Equipment always runs on the inverter Best protection, louder and less efficient; for server rooms

For computers, NAS systems and servers with modern power supplies, choose a model with a pure sine wave output. Some power supplies react badly to the cheaper stepped output on battery.

How Big Does It Need to Be?

Network equipment is frugal. Typical values:

  • Fibre modem or cable modem: 5–15 W
  • Router: 10–20 W
  • Small switch: 10–20 W, more with PoE access points or cameras
  • Two‑bay NAS: 20–40 W

A home network rarely needs more than 50–80 W. Even a small UPS with around 600 VA usually keeps that running for twenty minutes or more, which covers the vast majority of outages. Check two numbers when buying: the wattage (W) must exceed your load, and the manufacturer's runtime chart shows how long it lasts at that load.

Worth knowing: For a router or modem alone, small DC battery packs that plug directly into the 12 V input are an inexpensive alternative. They cannot protect a NAS, though.

Shut Down Cleanly

For a NAS or server, the UPS should not only bridge outages but also announce when the battery runs low. Connect it with the supplied USB cable: most NAS systems recognise the UPS automatically and shut down safely before the battery is empty. With the open‑source tool Network UPS Tools (NUT), one NAS can even tell other devices on the network to shut down as well.

Things to Avoid

  • Do not plug in heavy loads. Laser printers, heaters and coffee machines draw short, very high currents and overload a small UPS. They belong on the wall socket.
  • Do not forget the battery. Lead‑acid batteries typically last three to five years. Run the built‑in self‑test regularly and replace the battery when runtime drops. Monitoring can remind you when a self‑test fails.
  • Do not daisy‑chain. Plugging a UPS into a power strip with surge protection, or another UPS, can cause faults. Use the wall socket.

Will the Internet Keep Working?

A UPS makes sure that your side stays up. Whether the connection itself survives a longer outage depends on the provider's equipment in the street and the building, which may or may not have its own backup power. For short dips and outages of a few minutes, the connection usually holds and the difference is dramatic: no restarts, no dropped calls, no checks.

For businesses that rely on telephony over the internet, card payments or remote access, this is especially important. A UPS for the network corner is one of the cheapest pieces of reliability you can buy. It belongs on the list of small business essentials.

Conclusion

Most power problems are short, and most network equipment needs little power, which makes a small line‑interactive UPS an easy win. Put the modem, router, switch and NAS on it, connect the NAS by USB for a clean shutdown, keep heavy loads off it and test the battery once in a while. The next flicker of the lights then passes without anyone noticing, including your network.
