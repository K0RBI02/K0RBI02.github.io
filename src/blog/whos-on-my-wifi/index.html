Who's on My Wi‑Fi? Making Sense of the Device List

Your router lists every device it has seen. Here is how to put a name to each entry, and what to do about the ones you can't place.

You open the router's admin page for once, click on the network overview and find twenty‑three devices. You own maybe twelve. Some have friendly names like Anna's iPhone, others are called esp_3a91f2 or just show a string of letters and numbers. Is the neighbour on your Wi‑Fi? Is something spying on you?

The good news: almost every mystery entry has a boring explanation, and with half an hour and a bit of method you can put a name to all of them.

What the Router Actually Knows

The device list is built from what each device tells the router when it joins. Understanding those few pieces of information makes the rest of this article much easier.

  • MAC address. A hardware address like a4:83:e7:12:34:56, used inside your local network. The first half traditionally identifies the manufacturer.
  • IP address. The local address the router hands out, usually something like 192.168.1.23. How that works is explained in IP addresses, DHCP and NAT.
  • Hostname. The name the device suggests for itself. Laptops and phones usually send something readable; cheap smart devices often send a chip name or nothing at all.
  • Connection type. Cable, 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz or a guest network, plus when the device was last seen.

Most routers also keep devices in the list for a while after they leave, which is why the count is often higher than what is actually online right now.

Why So Many Entries Look Strange

Private MAC Addresses

Phones, tablets and many laptops no longer show their real hardware address to every network. iPhones and iPads call it Private Wi‑Fi Address, Android uses a randomised MAC by default, and Windows offers random hardware addresses as an option. It is a privacy feature: shops and public hotspots can't follow your phone from place to place.

At home it has two side effects. The manufacturer lookup no longer works, because the address is made up, and some settings make a device appear with a new address now and then, so one phone can show up as two or three entries over time.

Worth knowing: You can recognise a made‑up address by its second character. If it is 2, 6, A or E (as in x2:…, xA:…), the address was set by software, not burned in by a manufacturer.

Devices You Forgot You Own

The usual suspects behind cryptic names:

  • Smart‑home gear: plugs, bulbs, thermostats, robot vacuums, doorbells. Many use the same few Wi‑Fi chips, which is where names like ESP_… or tuya_… come from. Our guide to smart‑home standards explains why some of them need Wi‑Fi at all.
  • Entertainment: TVs, streaming sticks, game consoles, soundbars, smart speakers.
  • The rest of the household: e‑readers, smartwatches, the printer, a car that updates itself in the driveway, the heat pump or solar inverter in the basement.
  • Your own network gear: mesh nodes, repeaters and powerline adapters often appear as devices too.
  • Visitors: the phone of a friend who stayed last weekend.

Identifying Every Entry, Step by Step

Work through the list once, properly. After that, keeping it tidy takes minutes.

  1. Start with the easy ones. Anything with a clear hostname gets a clear name in the router: Kitchen speaker, Lena's laptop. Most routers let you rename devices directly in the list.
  2. Check the manufacturer. For entries with a real MAC address, the router often shows the vendor already. If it doesn't, an online MAC vendor lookup turns the first six characters into a company name. A Chinese chip maker usually means a smart‑home gadget.
  3. Compare addresses with the devices. Phones show their Wi‑Fi address in the Wi‑Fi details of the network you are connected to. TVs, printers and consoles have it somewhere under network settings.
  4. Use the timeline. "Last seen" and connection time are clues: a device that appears every evening at seven is probably the TV, one that comes and goes with a family member is their phone.
  5. Unplug and watch. For the stubborn ones, switch suspected devices off one by one and see which entry goes offline. Old‑fashioned, but it always works.
  6. Clean up. Remove entries for devices that no longer exist, so the next review starts from a clean list.

Quick win: Give important devices a fixed address in the router (often called DHCP reservation or always assign the same IP address). Their entries stay stable, and the printer and NAS are always where you expect them.

An app such as Fing, a free network scanner for your phone, can help with step 2 and 3 by listing devices and guessing their type. It sees the same things the router sees, so treat its guesses as hints, not answers.

What the Signs Mean

Once most entries have a name, the remaining ones usually fall into one of these patterns:

What you see Likely explanation Verdict
Same phone listed several times Private or rotating MAC addresses Harmless, delete the old entries
Cryptic name, chip maker as vendor Smart‑home device Find it, name it, consider the guest network
Device only on the guest network A visitor Fine, that is what the guest network is for
Unknown device on cable Something plugged into a socket or switch Worth a walk around the house
Unknown device on Wi‑Fi, online for days Forgotten device or someone with your password Investigate, then change the password if unsure
Lots of unknown devices appearing at once Shared password, or a repeater bridging another network Change the password now

When Something Really Doesn't Belong

If an entry stays a mystery after all the steps above, assume your Wi‑Fi password has travelled further than you meant it to. That happens more often than break‑ins: it was shared with a babysitter, written on the fridge, or synced to a guest's phone, which then offered it to their friends.

The fix is the same either way:

  1. Change the Wi‑Fi password to a long, new one. Every device has to reconnect, which also shows you which ones you still use.
  2. Check the security mode: WPA3, or WPA2/WPA3 mixed, and WPS switched off. The router checklist covers these settings in fifteen minutes.
  3. Change the router's admin password too, if it was ever the default or written down.
  4. Move visitors and gadgets to their own network. A guest network for visitors and a separate one for smart‑home devices keeps the main network small and easy to read, as described in segmenting your home network.

Rule of thumb: Blocking an unknown device by its MAC address is a tidy‑up, not a lock. Addresses are visible and easy to fake. The password and the security mode are what actually keep people out.

For Small Businesses

In an office, the device list is also an inventory. Name devices after a scheme (rec‑printer, meet‑room‑tv), keep staff phones and visitors on the guest network, and look at the list at least once a quarter and whenever someone leaves the company, as part of offboarding. If you want alerts instead of manual checks, monitoring for small networks can tell you when a new device appears.

Conclusion

Those twenty‑three devices were never a mystery, just a list nobody had sorted. Name the obvious ones, look up the manufacturers, watch the timeline, unplug the stubborn ones, and expect phones to appear more than once because of private addresses. If something still doesn't belong, change the Wi‑Fi password, check the security settings and give guests and gadgets their own network. After that, a two‑minute look at the list every few months keeps you in the picture.
